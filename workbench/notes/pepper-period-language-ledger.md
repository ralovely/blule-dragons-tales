Indigo is born in 1901, British, bilingual through her French mother, scientifically trained, and internationally travelled. Her trips are T1 (1938–39), T2 (1948), T3 (1955–56), T4 (1963), T5 (1971). The [plan](../plans/editorial-revision-plan.md), [canon](../../reference/canon-ledger.md) and [chronology](../../reference/chronology.md) govern her biography, not the comparators below.

**Attested** means the quoted wording occurs in the identified dated source. It does not mean first use, common use, or automatic suitability for Indigo. **Suitable** is an editorial recommendation. **Unresolved** means this search did not establish the claim; it does not mean anachronistic. Dates below belong to letters, speeches or editions, not digitisation dates.

The most useful private comparator is Freya Stark's 1928 correspondence: a British multilingual woman traveller, born in 1893, but not a scientist or a surrogate Indigo. It is a published, edited selection, consulted in OCR rather than checked against every manuscript letter or page image. Its evidence is stronger for constructions than for original punctuation. Dorothy Hodgkin supplies scientific discourse, not private diary style; Hansard establishes public vocabulary, not women's intimate speech. No evenly sampled longitudinal corpus was assembled. In particular, private 1940s–1971 correspondence remains underrepresented. Recommendations for retaining early habits into T5 are reasoned character choices, not measured cohort frequencies.

## Small working palette

Use these as models of what a sentence does, not quotations to paste. No entry needs a period marker. Contractions can accompany precision; elaborate syntax and short exclamations can coexist. All source IDs resolve below.

| *Mon Dieu !* | Académie, eighth edition (1935), **dieu**, paragraph listing exclamations [S5] | Surprise, impatience, worry or pain, according to context. Available T1–T5. | An exclamation, not a declaration of religious belief. Least necessary candidate: repeated use risks caricature. |

**Implementation selection:** following the author's request for more audible dating, use a few marked English turns alongside quiet syntactic changes, rather than distributing markers across every entry. The six applications are recorded in the change log. No French is inserted: prefer it when memory, an addressee or a thought explains the switch. The protected *Deux vies* in 77 is untouched; this ledger does not claim a dated attestation of that exact manuscript formulation.

Across the five trips, preserve a recognisable speaker. T1 can record the route from observation to tentative explanation; T2 can apply those habits to established routines; T3 can retain exactness alongside grief and impatience; T4 can turn established judgement towards her adult children; T5 can omit explanation she no longer needs while still qualifying what she cannot know. These are applications of the agreed voice matrix, not historical rules about how women aged. The early sources do not expire in 1939; later sources do not retrospectively prove earlier usage.

## Second World War references — restored and author-approved

Originally researched at the author's request, then mistakenly shelved when the agent interpreted the wording emphasis too broadly. The author subsequently asked for the coupons reference and anything else omitted. Both references below are now proposed in T2, with review status cleared for 32 and 60 only. The author rejected an explanation of dragons' non-involvement in the war: they are animals, not human-commanded participants. No such explanation, wartime service, location or family experience is added. Neither historical reference belongs in T1, which ends before the war.

| Reference and attested language | Primary evidence, date and locator | Possible use and limits |
|---|---|---|
| Clothes coupons; “Make Do and Mend” | Ministry of Information, *Make Do and Mend*, **1943** newsreel trailer, preserved by IWM. In the [IWM transcript](https://www.iwm.org.uk/history/second-world-war/home-front/rationing/make-do-and-mend-1943), the opening archive dialogue says “we haven't got enough coupons!”; the closing dialogue proposes a “Make Do and Mend group”. These are period film words, distinct from the modern curator's commentary. IWM's accompanying historical account dates British clothes rationing from June 1941 to 1949. | A light allusion to the value of clothes could sharpen Indigo's reaction to the laundry theft in **60 (July 1948)**, or the mending already present in **33 (September 1948)** could prompt recognition of the campaign. Choose one location, not both. Clothes rationing is still current in 1948: do not imply coupons vanished on VE Day. A remembered slogan is less biographically demanding than asserting that Indigo attended a class, sewed uniforms or endured a particular shortage. The film is government persuasion, not proof that everyone welcomed the campaign. In T3–T5, use only as recollection or a retained habit, not current British clothes rationing. |
| The liberation of Paris, **25 August 1944**; “Paris libéré” | Charles de Gaulle, [Hôtel de Ville speech, 25 August 1944](https://www.charles-de-gaulle.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Discours-de-lHotel-de-Ville-de-Paris.pdf), transcription held by the Fondation Charles de Gaulle. In the speech's opening sequence: “Paris ! Paris outragé ! Paris brisé ! Paris martyrisé ! mais Paris libéré !” Later: “L'ennemi chancelle mais il n'est pas encore battu.” Locator: the first two substantive speech paragraphs, after the italic historical introduction. | **32 is already dated 25 August 1948**, exactly four years later. Its existing attention to the world beyond Aizomea makes a short recollection of the news worth considering, with the established Montmartre childhood connection giving it personal weight. A fragment such as the attested “Paris libéré” could carry that weight without a history lesson, but leave the speech's public-oratorical cadence out of Indigo's ordinary prose. Do not assert she was there, heard a particular broadcast, had relatives under occupation, or participated in the Resistance. Liberation of Paris is not the end of the war. Do not turn the returning injured dragons into an allegory for wartime victims or disturb the protected “She did.” ending. |

**Current applications:** 60 adds “One of my shirts was in it. I had saved up my clothing coupons to buy that shirt. Now a dragon was sitting on it.” The author found the initial “I had not spent clothes coupons to line a dragon's nest” obscure; the revision makes the purchase and the dragon's use explicit. This adds a small personal stake to the existing laundry incident; the purchase's date and place are unspecified. 32 opens “Four years today since Paris was liberated. I have been thinking of Montmartre.” This uses the existing dateline and childhood connection, without placing Indigo at the liberation or inventing a particular broadcast she heard. Both are newly composed prose supported by historical facts, not historical quotations. The current references establish neither Indigo's wartime whereabouts nor her family's experiences. Those remain open. The unused slogan and French quotation are not extra insertions owed to the manuscript.

## Sources and reproducible locators
