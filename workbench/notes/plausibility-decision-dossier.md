## A · Prologue: the international Dragon Protection Act

**Current fact.** In 1972 Indigo “helped create the Dragon Protection Act, an international law that recognised dragons as living beings deserving of conservation.” The preceding photograph places her outside the United Nations, not inside a recorded legislative session. No sentence says the General Assembly enacted the Act, that it binds every country, or when a treaty entered into force. Jamie is retrospectively summarising her public work. The photograph is undated; it is not evidence of a pre-1945 UN visit.

**Evidence and classification.** R2 correctly left the legal mechanism for R7. Re-reading its primary source confirms that Charter articles 10/13 concern recommendations and the development/codification of international law [S1]. That does not establish a general world-parliament power. But it is insufficient to conclude that an international instrument cannot be called an Act: the UN Treaty Collection explicitly says “no precise nomenclature exists” and that a title “has normally no overriding legal effects” [S2, Introduction]. This second source tests, rather than merely repeats, the initial objection. The current text does not assert the suspect enactment procedure. **Possible reader inference, not a confirmed contradiction.**

The supplied fictional source expressly names the **Dragon Protection Act of 1972** in IDs **128/147/160/196/205**, covering venom harvesting, humane working conditions, hunting, sanctions and endangered-species protection. Those rows establish neither jurisdiction nor a UN enactment procedure. Keep the name and year; do not replace the Act with a real conservation treaty or import those detailed provisions. This is fictional history within a world where dragons are publicly known, not a claim that real-world 1972 legislation protected dragons.

**Smallest option.** No change recommended. If the author finds the institutional inference distracting, replace only **“an international law” → “an international agreement”**. This retains the title, date, international reach, conservation purpose and Indigo's contribution, while suggesting cooperation rather than a world legislature. Tradeoff: “agreement” sounds less forceful and clarifies an underlying legal form the manuscript has not specified. It is an **optional canon clarification requiring explicit selection**, not a verified correction or an automatically authorised wording edit. Do not soften it further to an aspiration or declaration, which could weaken the existing law.

**Larger alternative.** None needed. Choosing signatories, ratification, national implementing Acts, a new UN power or a dragon-governance institution would add canon without solving a demonstrated story problem.

**Dependencies / R8 handoff.** P's complete advocacy paragraph and following public/private-work distinction; 32's hunting, scars and protected “(Editor's note: She did.)”; ledger chronology/payoff 10; timeline's 1972 anchor; source IDs above. Preserve “won an argument with a building” verbatim. R8 owns Jamie's voice, not a retrospective legal-system invention. If the alternative is selected, R8 should inherit that exact choice; otherwise its baseline remains unchanged. No R8 prose is drafted here.

## C · 24: hot-air launch

**Current fact.** In “The Earth's Hiccup”, metal platforms stand over “geysers that erupt with hot air instead of water”; a launch chamber channels a noisy column of air that shoots an insect-like glider upward for hundreds of feet before it tips into a glide. The same complete entry includes a dragon-wing vortex and a tree slingshot. It ends with a conversation with the wind: “The physics followed.”

**Evidence and classification.** The launch is not described as a hot-air balloon or as buoyancy from warm air alone. The forceful column, chamber and subsequent glide already distinguish it from simply standing over a warm vent. Calling this an ordinary, safe real-world geothermal launch would be unsupported; calling the explicitly extraordinary island phenomenon an internal contradiction is unsupported too. “Geysers … instead of water” announces the departure rather than concealing it. **Deliberate fantastic technology, not a demonstrated mechanics collision.** No calculation of pressure, wing loading or human acceleration is warranted without a decision to make the scene engineering-realistic.

**Smallest wording option.** Retain. No extra “updraught” sentence or safety qualification: it would restate the visible column without earning the technical tone. Replacing air with steam would create a different heat/scalding problem and change the phenomenon, not clarify it.

**Canon-changing option.** None needed. Do not give the blue metal a levitation property, borrow 34's floating eggs as a mechanism, restore R6's discarded causal explanation, add a dragon launcher to this section or turn gliding into permitted dragon riding.

**Dependencies.** Whole 24, neighbouring 23/25, 07's floating geography, 09's harvest-only riding, 34's now-unexplained buoyancy and the R6 record. The launch methods provide comic variety and independent human flight; all remain intact. Source ID 239's thermal-gliding dragon is compatible context, not proof of this human machine or a mechanism to insert.

**Current fact.** The Manaïari call them Mouelleubleus; Manami translates “roughly” as “the soft blue ones”, hears an unimpressed cat in it and suspects a child coined it. Pepper agrees with the suspicion. Neither the child's identity nor the word's actual origin is established. The French-looking wordplay sits in a bilingual narrator's notebook. 04 explicitly says she invents spellings as she transcribes an unwritten language; it also describes dragon-sound loanwords. These are existing framing facts, not proof of this particular word's etymology.

**Evidence and classification.** A reader may hear French; that resemblance does not prove a French borrowing, an impossibility in a fictional oral language, or that Manami is mistranslating. Japanese-derived personal names are not a universal naming law (ledger §2); 06/14's settler history does not make Manaïari simply historical Japanese. The Wyrmspan guidance explicitly permits naming freedom; no relevant source row located establishes an obligatory alternative name. **Possible linguistic distraction, not a confirmed contradiction.** No real-world dictionary can adjudicate an invented Manaïari etymology.

An added French-comparison sentence would explain the joke and distract from the dust-covered sandwich.

**Canon-changing option.** None needed. Do not make Nangula or Pepper the coiner, invent a French-speaking settler, identify the child, impose an island-wide naming rule or manufacture a loanword history. All require author approval and more dependent changes than the concern earns.

## Supporting research: bounded primary-source check

Consulted 31 October 2026; read full pages, not search-result summaries.

- **S1 — United Nations Charter, Chapter IV, articles 10 and 13 (1945).** [Official text](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/un-charter/chapter-4). Rechecked the exact source R2 cites. Article 10: the Assembly “may make recommendations”; 13: it initiates studies and recommendations encouraging international law's development and codification. Establishes those powers, not a prohibition on an instrument's title, the fictional Act's enactment procedure or an impossible UN photograph.

The Introduction expressly rejects a precise nomenclature and explains that title normally has no overriding legal effects. The Treaties section defines the generic category regardless of designation. This is present-day institutional guidance on legal categories/practice, **not a dated 1972 attestation of this fictional title**. Its account dates the 1969 Vienna Convention's entry into force to 1980; do not cite that Convention as already in force in 1972. No manuscript treaty history is inferred from it.
