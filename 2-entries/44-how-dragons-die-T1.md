*17th August 1938*

I have been asking how dragons die. It is not a question the Manaïari enjoy, though not because it is sad. It is because the answer is not one answer.

Some fade. Over weeks, they grow quieter. They eat less. Their colour pales. The Listeners say the dragon's presence thins, until one morning the flank is cool. It is not sudden, and not violent. It is a long exhale that, at some point, does not draw back in.

Some go to the sea. The dying dragon walks to the coast, enters the water, and swims out past the reef. The marine dragons rise to meet it. What happens beyond the fog line, nobody has seen. The fishermen say the water glows for a night afterwards, a deep blue, and then it stops.

Some choose. An old dragon will fly to a floating island, alone, without announcement, and not come back. The Manaïari do not follow. They watch from below until the shape disappears into the sky. There is no ceremony. There is no search. If the dragon wanted company, it would not have gone alone.

Rarest of all, a few turn to stone. It is slow, spanning decades. The tail stiffens first. Then the hind legs. Then the rest, creeping forward over years. The dragon lives through it, moving less and less, until one day it is still. What remains looks like a rock formation, a ridge in the shape of a sleeping animal. I have walked past two without realising what they were. Manami pointed them out. Once you see it, you cannot unsee it. The hills here have more faces than I thought.

And then there are the ones who, the Manaïari say, do not die at all. They become part of the island. The stone. The hum. The blue in the mountains. I asked Manami if she believed this. She looked at me as if I had asked whether she believed in the ground she was standing on. Nangula died three years ago, in a bed in Namibia, far from here. But I think part of her is in these mountains. I think she would have wanted that.
