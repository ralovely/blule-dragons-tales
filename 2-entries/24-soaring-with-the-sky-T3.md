*21st September 1955*

**The Dragon's Sneeze**

I stood on a cliff today and very nearly forgot to breathe. Below, a young woman stood at the edge, clad in a harness of leather and shimmering blue metal ribs. She was waiting for the dragon.

A huge dragon lumbered up from the valley floor and launched into the sky with a downbeat of its wings that shook the rock I stood on. And in that precise moment, the woman simply... fell. For a terrifying second, she plunged towards the rocks. But then the vortex of air left by the dragon caught her like a dandelion seed and threw her upwards, laughing, into the sun. The dragon glanced back.

**The Village Fling**

In the canyons on the other side of the spire, they don't ask the dragons or the earth for a lift. They ask the trees. The Manaïari have woven a monstrous slingshot between two ancient, rubbery trees. It takes a whole team of laughing men and women, chanting in rhythm, to pull the thing taut.

Today's flyer was a grandmother, strapped into the leather pouch with the calm dignity of someone boarding a train. An elder woman gave a final shout, the rope was cut, and with a deep, resonant *thrum* that vibrated in my chest, the grandmother was flung into the sky. Her delighted 'Wheee!' echoed off the canyon walls.

**The Earth's Hiccup**

We travelled to the smoking plains, where the air smells of sulphur and creation. Here, flight is a far more serious, punctual affair. The Manaïari have built intricate metal platforms over geysers that erupt with hot air instead of water.

I watched a man in a sleek, insect-like glider stand perfectly still in a launch chamber. The ground began to tremble. A low rumble grew, and then with a deafening *WHOOSH*, a column of air shot him vertically into the sky. He went straight up, a rocket of bone and leather, for hundreds of feet before his machine tipped into a silent, effortless glide. Less a village fête, this one. A piece of clockwork powered by the island's own hiccup.

**A Conversation with the Wind**

However they leave the ground, it strikes me now, the launch is the unimportant part. It is merely the opening line of a conversation.

The Manaïari attend to the air as they fly. I saw the grandmother from the canyon adjust the tips of her glider's wings, just slightly, to ask a thermal for a little more height. I saw the woman from the cliff lean into a crosswind, listening to its suggestion before offering her own.

Their gliders, with those intricate blue metal frames, seem to be extensions of their own senses. The dragons, I believe, taught them a language of air. The physics followed.

I asked Manami if she flies. She looked at me as if I'd asked whether she breathes.
