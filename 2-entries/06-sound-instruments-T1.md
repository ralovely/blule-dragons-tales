*27th July 1938*

The Listeners use touch. But there is a second tradition, older and more public, that uses sound.

The instruments are simple. Hollowed seashells that produce a single clear note. Smooth river stones clicked together in rhythms. Feathers drawn across taut string to make a hum so faint I can barely hear it, though the dragons hear it from across a valley. And gourds, grown from seeds the Japanese settlers carried with them, dried and filled with seeds or pebbles or, in the rarest cases, blue stone dust. A gourd filled with blue stone dust sounds like the voice of the stone, bottled.

Each instrument reaches a different kind of dragon. The seashell carries over water. The stones travel through rock into caves. The feather-hum is for the small, shy species that flee from anything louder. Conversation is the wrong word. A fisherman blows a shell and a sea dragon changes course. A child clicks stones at a cave entrance and a dragon inside shifts to make room. Call and response, but the response is movement, not sound.

I tried the seashell. The note I produced was, I was told, technically correct. The sea dragon surfaced, looked at me, and submerged again. Manami took the shell, blew what sounded like the same note, and the dragon came alongside the boat. "It's not the note," she said. "It's the breath behind it."

One evening I heard a gourd played at dusk, a low rattling sequence I had not heard before. Three dragons wheeling high above the valley turned and came back to the village. I asked what the sequence meant. "Dinner," someone said.
