I hired a boat in Wellington. A small fishing vessel, a crew of three. I told them I was looking for an island that did not appear on any chart. The captain looked at me, looked at the compass, and named a price that suggested he thought I was mad but preferred to be paid for his trouble. Fair enough.

For the first four days the eye pulled south-west. You hold it up, look through it, and the world in one direction is ever so slightly brighter than the rest. More vivid. One slice of the horizon, somehow more real than the rest. You turn until the colours sing, and that is your heading. On the fifth morning the bright patch swung north. I adjusted the course. On the sixth it swung back. On the seventh it pointed, briefly and with apparent conviction, straight down. The captain stopped asking questions after that. He just followed whatever direction I gave him and smoked his pipe. N.B. I logged each morning's heading. Laid out in a row, they make a drunkard's compass rose.

I know now that we were being misdirected. The island's defences do not begin at the fog. They begin much further out, in the currents, in the confusion, in the gentle suggestion that perhaps you have made a mistake and should turn around. The compass was fighting through it. Some days it won. Some days the island did.

On the eleventh day we hit the fog. Thick, white, absolute. The crew grew quiet, then drowsy. I remember the youngest one sitting down mid-sentence and closing his eyes. Then the captain. Then the other. I held the compass to my eye and tried to stay awake. Through the glass, the world ahead was blazing with colour. I followed it as long as I could.

I woke in a stone room. The compass was in my hand, closed.

It took me three days to find out what happened to the crew. When Manami finally understood what I was asking, she looked almost amused. The ship had been carried safely past the island and set down in open water. The crew would have woken refreshed, confused, and with no memory of the fog. They were fine.
