*15th September 1948*

The coast is where the secret ends. You can stand on the beach and see the wall of mist a few hundred yards out, a clean line between this world and the one I came from. I go there sometimes when I need to remember that the rest of the world exists. It helps less than it used to.

I am not always alone when I come. There is a young man I see sometimes, standing at the water's edge with his gaze fixed somewhere beyond the horizon. Manami says a few leave. Most come back.

The children here learn to swim before they can walk. I mean that literally. I watched a man place a baby in shallow water and it moved with a confidence that made me, a grown woman who learnt to swim at nine in a municipal pool in Surrey, feel personally inadequate. The sea is their garden.

Fishing is done in partnership with the sea dragons, working alongside them as a farmer works with a sheepdog. The dragons herd fish towards the boats, and the fishermen read their movements to know where to cast. I sat in a boat and watched the whole exchange. I saw only splashes and adjustments, then a catch so large the boat sat low on the way home. The first fish of the day goes to the nearest dragon. I asked a fisherman why. "Manners," he said. On the way back in, the crew sang. I am starting to think silence during work is considered rude here. The harvesters sing, the fishermen sing, the weavers sing. Even the mending women keep a tune going while they settle an argument. Only the Listeners are quiet, and that is their job.

The tides here do not follow the moon the way I expect. The Manaïari say the sea dragons affect the currents. Whether this is fact or folklore, I cannot tell. I have stopped trying to distinguish between the two.

There are dragons out there that never come ashore. I see them from the cliffs sometimes, vast shapes moving just below the surface, turning the water a shade darker as they pass. The legends say some of them are older than the island itself. At night, their calls carry up the cliff face. It sounds like whalesong, but deeper, slower. Older, somehow.

Even the sand has a faint blue cast in certain lights. The shells too. The whole coastline looks like a painting someone mixed slightly too much blue into. And sometimes, after a storm, things wash up. Driftwood furred with barnacles from distant oceans. A length of rope. Once, a child's shoe. The Manaïari gather these from the tideline and keep them. Fragments of a world they know exists but have chosen not to join.

Once, I am told, a boat came round from the south on the morning current, weathered and empty. No one said what had become of its sailor, but the guess makes itself: a dragon is, after all, a very large animal with an appetite. It is the only thing the sea has brought that they did not keep. They floated it back out on the evening tide, pointed the way it came.
