*18th June 1948*

To the Manaïari, a shared meal can solve almost anything. A diplomatic incident, a broken heart, a philosophical impasse: all are best addressed around a hearth. Their entire culture seems to be a grand, joyful conspiracy of food. Harvesting it, preparing it, serving it.

I visited a restaurant yesterday with Manami, carved directly into a cliff face. She ordered for both of us without asking. Mon Dieu, the food! Humans occupied the terraces while their dragon companions perched on wider ledges below, their magnificent heads rising up to become part of the dinner party. The heat from the dragons rose gently through the stone, warming the terraces from below, and conversation bounced off the rock walls in a cheerful, incomprehensible roar. The kitchen behind it was communal, enormous, with stone counters at two heights.

The menu is a wonderful kind of madness. I was served a dish of rice and phosphorescent moss, cooked on a vast, hot stone by a man who conducted the whole operation with the focus of a surgeon. When the moment came, he raised a hand, and the dragon behind him exhaled a gentle, warm breath that seared the moss, causing it to glow with a final, brilliant pulse. Neither of them needed to look at the other. It tastes of warm soil and starlight.

Then there are the berries. An ice dragon flash-freezes them with a puff of frigid air, creating a confection that is impossibly frozen on the outside with a molten, sun-hot core. A paradox on a spoon. There is also a seasoning made from dragon venom (a few drops only), which tastes, as near as I can put it, of the colour yellow. I am aware that makes no sense. It is accurate nonetheless.

By the end of the evening, a dragon had fallen asleep with its chin on a terrace railing, and a child had claimed its lower lip as a shelf for her pudding bowl.
