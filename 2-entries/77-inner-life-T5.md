*12th September 1971*

I wonder sometimes if I am selfish. I chose this. I left my family for a world of dragons. I know most people would not understand. I am not sure I understand. I tell myself I am doing this for science. I am not entirely lying.

The difficulty is that I love it here. I am happy. And every moment of happiness is undercut by the knowledge that it is built on absence. I am happy because I left. That equation has never balanced, and I have stopped trying to make it.

I have known Manami for over thirty years now. She was the first person who spoke to me here, and she may know me better than anyone alive. She has never once asked me to stay. She has never once asked me to explain why I leave. I think that is what friendship looks like when it has had enough time to stop performing.

There is a particular loneliness in knowing something extraordinary and being unable to tell anyone.

I walked to the ridge this morning, to my rock. It was warm, as always. I sat for a long time. My knees ache more than they used to. The climb is harder. I did not bring my brushes or my notebook. I just sat, and looked, and tried to memorise the colour of the light.

River has changed since we arrived. She moves differently here. She laughs more easily. She looks at this place as someone who has found where she was always meant to stand. I recognise this because I felt it too, thirty years ago, and I know what it costs. She does not know yet what it will cost. A mother sees this coming and cannot say a word.

We are packing to leave. There was a new shell on my desk this morning. A tiny one, white, perfectly spiralled. The row along the windowsill now stretches from one end to the other. I packed them all.

River is folding something carefully into her bag, something she did not arrive with. A piece of cloth, dark blue, that I have not seen before. She held it to her face for a moment before wrapping it. The sky outside is clear. We should have good weather for the crossing.

*Deux vies.* Two lives. I cannot have them at the same time. This is the only thing about Aizomea that hurts. (I am not counting the claws, the fire, or the tail the width of an oak tree that I must walk past every morning on my way to breakfast.)
