*22nd March 1956*

I spent an entire day following one. It moved so slowly I had to remind myself it was moving at all, a vast, low creature whose back had become a meadow. Grasses grew along its spine. Tiny white flowers I couldn't identify clustered between the shoulder blades. A sapling, no taller than my forearm, leaned slightly with each step, correcting itself as the dragon shifted weight, like a sailor riding a swell. Butterflies orbited it. I counted seventeen species of plant in an hour and stopped counting only because I ran out of page.

The relationship, I have come to understand, is tightly reciprocal. The algae that coat the lower scales clean them, slowly digesting the grime and parasites that would otherwise accumulate. The mushrooms that sprout along the flanks do something similar, breaking down dead scale and returning nutrients to the living surface beneath. The tiny insects pollinate the flowers, and the flowers produce a nectar that pools in the grooves between scales. Everything feeds everything else. It is biology so circular that you could watch it for a week and still not be sure who is doing whom the favour.

At night, everything changes. I went back after dark and found the dragon asleep, perfectly still, and its back had become something else entirely. The algae glowed. A pale blue-green light traced every scale. The mushrooms gave off their own faint luminescence, a warmer tone, and between them tiny insects darted and paused and darted again, each one carrying its own small spark. There was an entire nocturnal world happening on the back of a sleeping animal, a country the size of a bus, running on its own clock, utterly indifferent to mine.

I should also mention the tenant. A very small dragon, no bigger than a cat, lives permanently on this creature's back. It has hollowed out a nest between two mushroom clusters and, as far as I can tell, never touches the ground. I asked a man nearby if they were related. "No," he said. "It just moved in one day. The big one didn't seem to mind." I watched the small one emerge at dusk, stretch, eat something off a mushroom cap, and disappear back into its nest. It has the air of someone who has found the perfect flat and has no intention of ever leaving.
