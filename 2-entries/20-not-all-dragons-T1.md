*5th October 1938*

I find myself staring south more and more these days, a rather pointless exercise. Manami has made it quite clear that the southern lands are not for me. She called it a warning, though its solemnity would have suited any law. I noticed her watching my reaction carefully, weighing what to tell me next by how I took it. Some dragons, she explained, do not share their northern cousins' curiosity for humankind. Nangula would have asked the better question. She always asked the one I had not thought to.

Manami calls them purists. They see Aizomea as a perfect, self-willed garden, and we humans are an unwelcome weed. Our very presence, our desire to build and order things, offends their love for the island's untamed state.

The border has no line on a map. The Manaïari call it the Tangle. It's a patchwork of defences: one stretch of forest buzzes with the quiet menace of giant, iridescent wasps; the next is filled with vines as thick as pythons that grasp at sound. There is, inexplicably, a mile-wide meadow of tiny yellow flowers that cause catastrophic hiccups. _(I got too close. Twenty minutes of my life I shall not get back.)_ It is a place woven from a hundred different kinds of 'No'.

The reason for this great rift is, like so many things here, a story. It's told more like a poem than a history, a tale from the time after the Manaïari had settled. When they first cleared a field for a farm, teaching wild grasses to grow in neat, straight lines, some dragons saw a clever partnership. They drew closer. But others saw only a cage for the grass, a prison for the land. And in silent, sorrowful disagreement, they left. As one leaves a room where the music has changed.

The southern coast receives only a thin wash of fog, enough to discourage a passing ship while leaving the continent visible. The southern dragons prefer it that way. They accept being seen; they demand to be left alone. The tales say a handful of humans have reached the southern shore over the centuries. Ambitious sailors, naive explorers, or the occasional fool with more courage than sense. None of them left. The tales do not say what happened to them. Nobody asks.

Manami finished her lesson with a story I haven't stopped thinking about. A generation ago, a child's ball rolled into the Tangle, caught by a thorny vine. The mother, knowing the rules, left a bowl of fresh milk at the border. The next morning, the ball was sitting on the grass. The milk was gone, and the empty bowl had been filled with a few colourful, fallen leaves. A bargain, it seems, was struck.
