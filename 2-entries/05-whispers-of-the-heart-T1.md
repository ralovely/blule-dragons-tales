This morning I watched a woman sit with her hand flat against a dragon's flank for the better part of an hour. Neither moved. The dragon's breathing was slow and deep, warm enough to shift the grass. When the woman finally stood, she walked straight to the village well, lowered a bucket, and brought it back. The dragon drank. Nobody had said a word.

Dragons do not speak. I need to write that down plainly, because I spent my first months here waiting for them to. I talked to dozens, in English, in French, in what little Manaïari I've picked up. They listened. They always listen. You would swear on your life they understand every syllable. But they never answer. Not in language. Not once.

The woman at the dragon's flank is what the Manaïari call a Listener. There are perhaps thirty in the village, women mostly, though not all. You can spot them by their hands: the palms are always warm, even on a cold morning. A Listener's handshake is unmistakable. She places her hand on a dragon and goes still, and when she rises, she knows things. What she knows arrives wordlessly: a shift in pressure, a movement of warmth, somewhere a yes or a no. I asked one what it was like. She thought for a long time. "Like hearing colour," she said, which clarified nothing and somehow everything.

Manami arranged for me to try. An old, patient dragon who apparently tolerates foreigners. I placed my palm on its side. Warm scales. I closed my eyes. I concentrated fiercely. I felt... a heartbeat. The vague sense of being near something very large and very calm. Nothing else. After twenty minutes, the dragon opened one eye, looked at me, and exhaled slowly through its nostrils. Even I could read that one. I said "sorry" before I could stop myself. (I have been saying "sorry" to everything on this island. Trees, doorframes, a rock I tripped over. The British affliction travels.) Manami was already laughing. "You think too loudly," she said. I wonder if Nangula was better at this. I suspect she was. She was better at most things that required silence.

But what fascinates me most is not what the dragons express. It's what they withhold. The Manaïari language contains fourteen separate words for different qualities of dragon silence. Fourteen. I have managed to learn seven. There is a word for the silence of a dragon that is resting, and a different word for the silence of a dragon that is pretending to rest. A word for the silence before a dragon acts, and another for the silence after it has decided not to. Manami tried to explain the remaining seven. She could not. "They can only be felt," she said. "Like the difference between two blues." She saw me looking at my fingertips while she said it, and smiled. The faint blue has deepened since I arrived; it does not wash off, and I cannot account for it. What, on this island, is dyeing me?
