*5th April 1938*

*Day twenty-eight.*

The language here sounds like water over stones. Lots of vowels, soft consonants, a rhythm that rises and falls without ever quite hurrying. It is, I think, a language designed to be overheard by sleeping dragons. There is even a formal register, spoken more softly and slowly, used only near dragons, out of respect for the Listeners who might be working nearby.

I am not starting from nothing. Manami speaks English, learnt from Nangula over her many visits, and a handful of others know enough to help me when I point at something and look lost. I am teaching Manami more in return, and she is a quicker student than I am. This is not a compliment to her so much as a confession about me.

I have been saying what I thought meant "good morning" for three weeks now. Manami told me yesterday that I have in fact been saying something closer to "the fish agrees." She waited three weeks. She says it was funnier that way. The children, who have been following me around repeating everything I say, have apparently known all along. They are still saying it. To each other. While looking at me.

The grammar is its own world. The verb "to be" does not work the way I expect. You do not say "I am hungry." You say, as near as I can render it, "hunger visits me." Emotions, states of mind, even weather: they are all visitors. They arrive. They stay. They leave. You do not own your sadness. It is passing through. I find this either profound or maddening, depending on the day.

There are things in this language that English cannot hold. There is no word for "own" or "possess." You can say "I use this" or "this stays with me" but not "this is mine." There is a tense that does not exist in English, something between past and present, used for things that happened but whose effects are still felt. Not "the dragon left" but nearer to "the dragon left and the leaving is still here." They use it for grief. For seasons. For love. I have no way to translate it. I have started using it anyway.

Some words are borrowed directly from dragon sounds. A low rumble that means something like "contentment" has entered the human vocabulary. People use it in conversation, dropping their voice into a register that is not quite speech and not quite growl. The first time I heard it, I thought the man speaking had a stomach complaint. He was telling someone he loved his house.

There is no written form. Everything is oral. I may be the first person to write Manaïari words down, scratching them into the margins of a notebook in a spelling system I am inventing as I go. N.B. The word-list stands at forty-one. Manami says eleven of them are wrong, and will not say which; she says finding out is good for me. I feel a low guilt about the whole enterprise, some days, like a woman pinning butterflies to a board and calling it love of the species.

Place names change. A hill is not named. It is called "the hill where the old dragon slept" until a new dragon sleeps there, and then it becomes something else. A path is "the path past the red flowers" until the flowers die, and then it is "the path where the red flowers were." The landscape is a living sentence, constantly being revised. I asked Manami for directions last week and she sent me to a landmark that had, apparently, been renamed the previous afternoon. I arrived somewhere else entirely. She did not see the problem.

The word for "blue" and the word for "home" are nearly identical. The tonal difference is so slight I cannot hear it. I keep saying one when I mean the other. No one corrects me. I am beginning to suspect that, here, it may not matter which one I mean.

I am learning this language the way one learns to swim: badly, in public, swallowing a great deal of water. Being bilingual helps; the French half of my brain is more willing to bend than the English half, which keeps insisting on rules. I catch myself thinking in it sometimes, just for a moment, before the English rushes back in. In those moments, the world here makes a kind of sense it does not make in my own tongue. I am not yet fluent. I may never be.

P.S. Coming home in the dark tonight, no lantern, I read the path through my boot soles. Warm, warmer, almost hot at the turning to the communal kitchen. The more a route is walked, the warmer its stones keep. I missed my own doorstep twice. It is the coldest thing on the street.
