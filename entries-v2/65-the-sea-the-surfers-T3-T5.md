The Manaïari surf the way the British drink tea: constantly, without ceremony, on the apparent principle that life without it would be technically possible but not worth the bother. Children surf before they can run properly. Old women surf. The harbour master surfs on her lunch break, props her board against the dock wall, and goes back to directing the fleet with wet hair and no comment. A man carried a board under one arm and a baby under the other, handed the baby to someone on the beach, and paddled out without breaking stride. He caught the first wave of the day and rode it the full length of the cove without once shifting his weight. Effortless. I suspect he does this every morning.

The boards are carved from a pale, buoyant wood I cannot identify, shaped with blue stone tools and waxed with dragon tallow. Each one is slightly different. Some are enormous, whole-family boards, ridden by three or four people at once, laughing, shifting their weight together. The boards are stored upright in racks near the beach, dozens of them, sun-bleached and salt-stained. They look like a row of standing stones. Some have been repaired so many times there is almost nothing of the original left. A board, like a dragon's nest, becomes a record of its use.

Dragons surf too. They ride the waves belly-down, wings tucked, using their tails as rudders. The long, serpentine species are naturals. The stocky, heavy ones are not, but they try anyway, with a determination that borders on fury. One wiped out so spectacularly it created a secondary wave that the Manaïari children rode back to shore. Everyone cheered, including the dragon.

They surf together. Humans and dragons, side by side, riding the same wave, adjusting to each other's speed and line. A dragon drops in and a surfer adjusts. A surfer takes off late and a dragon slows to match. It is the same wordless conversation I see everywhere on this island, but faster, more physical, more joyful. The best of them ride in tandem, the human on a board, the dragon gliding just beneath the surface alongside, and they carve the same turn at the same moment.

There are waves that only dragons can ride. Enormous, deep-ocean swells that break against the outer cliffs. The Manaïari sit on the rocks and watch them as you would a sunset. The dragons ride them for what looks like hours, carving lines across faces of water the height of buildings.

Every morning, before the fishing starts, the first surfers are in the water. It is the quietest part of the day. I went once, at Manami's insistence. I cannot surf. I sat on the board in the shallows and watched the others. The water was warm. A dragon surfaced beside me, looked at me, and dived under again. I stayed for an hour. The bay at Saint-Jean-de-Luz was never like this. Nothing was. I did not catch a wave. I caught something else.

River, on her second visit, took to it immediately. She says she is learning. From what I can see, she has already learnt. Kai takes her out at dawn. They come back wet and grinning and speaking a language I do not think is entirely Manaïari.
