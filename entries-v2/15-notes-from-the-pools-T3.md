There are rules. No one wrote them down, but everyone knows them. You do not splash. You do not enter a pool already occupied by a sleeping dragon. You do not, under any circumstances, comment on the noises a dragon makes when it first lowers itself into hot water. I will not attempt to describe these noises. I will say only that they are prolonged, involuntary, and that every dragon, without exception, looks mildly embarrassed afterwards. The Manaïari pretend not to hear.

The Manaïari have their own pools, smaller, heated by the same fire dragons. The dragons who tend these pools treat the task with a care that surprised me. One tested the water with the tip of its tail before a group of children climbed in, adjusting the temperature with a breath so gentle it barely stirred the surface. It held that temperature for an hour, perfectly steady, watching the children with one half-closed eye. Nobody thanked it. None needed to.

In the warm pools on the eastern slopes, there are critters. I don't know what else to call them. They are translucent, roughly the size of a fist, and they drift on the surface like soap bubbles with nowhere to be. When popped, they release a puff of gas that smells of strawberries and affects
dragons the way a good joke makes a child laugh: instantly, totally, without any dignity whatsoever. Certain dragons will sit in a pool for hours, popping them one after another with the glazed focus of someone shelling peanuts. Pop, giggle, reach, pop, giggle, reach. A creature the size of a house, reduced to helpless wheezing by a soap bubble. The Manaïari children find this spectacle funnier than the critters themselves. I cannot blame them.

The children have also worked out that if you throw a critter at a dragon, it pops on impact and the dragon laughs whether it wanted to or not. This has become a game. The dragons pretend to be furious, but the performance is unconvincing. One dragon keeps positioning itself suspiciously close to the children's throwing range, facing the other way, tail conveniently still. It deceives no one, least of all the children.
