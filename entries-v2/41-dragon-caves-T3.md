Each cave is shaped by its occupant the way a chair is shaped by the person who sits in it for forty years. The walls are worn smooth where a flank has pressed against them nightly. The floor is scooped where a tail curls. You can walk into an empty cave and know, from the grooves and the faint smell, what lived there and roughly how long ago it left.

There are, broadly, three kinds. High caves in the mountain faces, open to the wind, favoured by the flying species. Forest caves at ground level, damp and overgrown, where the entrance is sometimes hidden by vines and you don't realise you've walked into someone's home until someone clears their throat. And deep caves, far underground, where the blue stone in the walls glows brighter the further you descend. I visited one that was lit entirely by the stone. No sunlight. No fire. Just blue, everywhere, as if I had walked inside a sapphire.

The walls are marked. Claw marks, cut deep into the rock, in patterns that repeat across different caves. I have spent weeks sketching them. Some are long, deliberate grooves, scored with obvious care. Others are clusters of short scratches, grouped in arrangements too regular to be accidental. In one cave I found what I can only describe as a mural --- an entire wall covered in overlapping marks of different depths and ages, the oldest nearly worn flat, the newest still sharp enough to catch the light. It looked like generations of dragons had been adding to the same surface, each one leaving its mark over or beside the last. Whether this is art, or territory, or record-keeping, or all three, I cannot say. Manami thinks they are scratching posts. I think Manami is being modest about what the dragons are capable of.

In the communal caves, where dozens sleep together, there is an etiquette I am still learning. Each dragon has its corner. The boundaries are invisible but absolute. Noise is tolerated up to a point. Snoring, apparently, is not grounds for eviction, but it is grounds for being nudged, repeatedly, until you roll over.

My favourite thing about the caves is the entrance. Dragons sit at the mouth of their caves in the evenings the way old women sit outside their houses in a village in the south of France. Watching. Commenting silently on passers-by. Occasionally rumbling at one another. They have favourite spots. They have routines. One dragon has sat in the same position at the same cave mouth every evening for, I am told, longer than anyone can remember. It is not guarding anything.
