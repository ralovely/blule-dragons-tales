It started with a colour. A blue so deep and resonant it seemed to hum. I first noticed it in the mountains: impossibly tall, thin spires, drawn out at the tips like thread off a spindle. At their base, the rock is pale, ordinary granite. But the higher one looks, the deeper the blue becomes, until the very peaks are a vivid ultramarine, stained by something no longer quite there. Whatever gave them that colour has long since risen further, into the sky. The mountains are just the memory of it, stretched thin and left behind.

But the blue is not only in the rock. It is everywhere.

It's in the subtle, iridescent sheen of the seasoning a Manaïari child playfully sprinkled on a sleeping dragon's snout. It's in the glow of the pendants worn by everyone, a private star against the skin. I've seen it in the glint of a cooking pot, or in the thimbles of seamstresses stitching festival cloaks with shed dragon scales.

The blue is more than a pigment. It is a presence. A stone, they tell me, worked between the sky-borne dragons and the earth-bound Manaïari; and though it is everywhere here, it is nowhere treated as ordinary. They handle it with a care close to reverence. It is the nearest thing to magic I have found on this island, and I have stopped pretending otherwise in these pages.

I find myself looking for it everywhere now, this thread of blue stitching the world together. I keep coming back to the colour. I have written about it three times in this journal (~~luminous~~ ~~singing~~ there is no word that holds it) and I expect I shall write about it a fourth. Let the repetition stand. It is the most honest description I have. The rock I carried to Prague, the one that hummed at dusk, the one Nangula knew before I did: it was an egg, I understand now, and not a stone at all. But it hummed with this same blue note, the thing that had been drawing me across the world before I had any name to give it. It was always this. I simply had the wrong word.

One of the harvesters let me hold a raw stone today. It was heavier than it looked, and cool; noticeably cool, the cold of altitude still on it. When I gave it back, my fingertips were blue. I licked my thumb and rubbed. Nothing. Manami laughed. She held up her own hands, palms out, a faint, permanent blue soaked into the skin like a dye that had forgotten how to leave. All the harvesters have them, she said. You can spot one across a crowded market by the colour of her hands. It doesn't wash off. It isn't meant to.

I spoke with them afterwards. They were just back, their faces flushed from the wind and altitude, carrying baskets filled with what looked like ordinary, dull blue rocks. It is only when you hold one that you feel a faint, latent vibration. I have taken three samples. One for the laboratory. Two because Cendre collects rocks and I cannot help myself. N.B. By my hand-scale the stone weighs half again what granite would at that size. I shall want a proper balance. I shall likely have to build one myself.

They tell me the true source of the stone lies within the floating islands that graze the sky. Deep inside caves hidden beneath the mountains that crown these airborne lands. Reaching them requires a dragon. In fact, this harvest is the only accepted reason for a human ever to ride one.

The partnership is born of necessity. A dragon's power to fly to these secluded caves, a human's gentle hand to prise the fragile stones from the earth without shattering them. The riders, all women, sing on the way up. I can hear them from the ground, fading as they climb. When they speak of the journey afterwards, their hands go still in their laps; they talk of the silence in the clouds, and of the cool, dark peace of the caves.

I should say clearly: this is the only reason a human ever rides a dragon. The only one. I have asked. I have hinted. I have, on one occasion, stood beside a particularly obliging-looking dragon and tried to look light. Nobody rides for pleasure, for transport, for sport, for war. A dragon is not a horse; it carries a human when the dragon sees a reason to, and the harvest is the reason it accepts. There are no others.

They would not take me with them. It is a rite I am not privy to, and I shall not ask twice. I can only stand on solid ground, examine the rough-hewn treasures they bring back, and wonder. What does the inside of a sky-mountain look like, where the blue is born? I have sketched four guesses tonight. They are certainly all wrong.
