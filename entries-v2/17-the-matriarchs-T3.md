**On Mending**

They have no courts of law here, no bewigged judges or stern-faced constables. When a dispute arises, it is not a case to be won, but a tear to be mended. The task falls to certain women, known for little more than their patience and their skill with a teapot. (I say teapot. It is in fact a shallow clay bowl of something thick, spiced, and savoury that no self-respecting Englishwoman would call tea. I call it tea. The ritual is the same.) They bring the aggrieved parties together not to argue, but to do. To repair a sail, to bake bread, to re-sole a perfectly good shoe that didn't need re-soling.
I watched one such mending yesterday. Two families, furious over a tree whose shade fell on the wrong garden, sat across from each other in stony silence. The mending woman set them to making honey cakes together. By the time the dough needed kneading, they were arguing about whether to add ginger. By the time the cakes came out, the tree had been forgotten entirely. They were now arguing about ginger. She seemed to consider this progress, and so, I confess, did I. Justice here is closer to darning than to judgement: a thing one does with the hands until the hole no longer shows.

**On Story-Weaving**

History in Aizomea is not written in books. It is woven. In a round, sun-drenched building at the heart of the village, the great tapestries hang from floor to ceiling. I grew up visiting the tapestries at Bayeux and Angers with my mother. Those told stories of conquest. These tell stories of weather, harvest, and the passage of dragons. Here are no dates, no dry accounts of kings and battles. Instead, there is the story of a great drought told in countless shades of brown thread, the joy of a good harvest in a burst of gold and green. The women who weave them are the island's memory. The men, I later learned, are its palette. They prepare the dyes, coaxing colours from bark, roots, and crushed shells, things I could not begin to identify. To learn of the past, one does not read; one runs a hand over the heavy weave of a dragon's passing, the soft fuzz of a hatching.

I spent an afternoon there, running my fingers along decades. One tapestry near the door has a curious bald patch in its centre, conspicuously re-woven in thread that doesn't quite match. I asked the weaver about it. She said it recorded an event the village had collectively decided to be embarrassed about, and had voted to unpick. She would not tell me what it was. The thread, she said, remembers even if they'd rather it didn't.

**Village Sketches**

- The Docks. The harbour is carved into a natural cove, the stone worn smooth by centuries. The boats are small and light, designed to be hauled by one person. No need for large vessels here - they never go far. The harbour master is a young woman who directs the entire fishing fleet with a small wooden flute. No shouting, no bells. A high trill sent one boat to the eastern quay; a low, warbling tune told another its nets needed repair. The whole affair was as quiet and orderly as a well-conducted orchestra. One boat came in late. She played a single, flat note. It sounded exactly like disappointment.

- The Marketplace. Two men, red-faced and puffing out their chests, were arguing over a patch of shade. A woman walking past with a child remarked, loud enough for them to hear, "It reminds one of the Tale of the Two Grumpy Beetles." Then she was gone. The men deflated where they stood and settled the matter between them. The most effective weapon here, it seems, is a well-aimed fable. (I first wrote *merchants* and *wares*; both words are wrong. Nothing here is bought or sold, and it unsettles me that my pen reaches so readily for the vocabulary of a market that does not exist.)

- The Stonemason's. Watched a master mason at work. Her apprentice, a strong young man, could not get a great lintel to sit correctly. She came over, laid her palm flat against the granite for a long moment, and then tapped a single spot. "It wants to rest its heart there," she said. The apprentice shifted his lever by an inch, and the stone settled into place with a gentle sigh.
