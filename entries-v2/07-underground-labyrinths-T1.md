The landscape of Aizomea is a bit otherworldly, not really following any geological pattern I was taught at university. It's like something has pulled the mountains up, like a child who's grown too much, too quickly. The mountains are tall but not menacing. They leave space for light to cover the valleys where most of the Manaïari live. Some of the ridges have an odd quality to them. Curves that look almost deliberate. I sketched one this morning that, from a certain angle, resembled a sleeping animal. Probably a coincidence.

The mountains are also home to gigantic caves. The climb to reach them is beastly, but one enters through tunnels worn smooth, probably by the passage of dragons over centuries, and forgets the legs immediately. The scale is humbling; the first gallery must be three hundred feet from floor to ceiling, and I paced the length --- four hundred and twelve paces --- before giving up on the height entirely. I caught myself arranging my sketches the way Cassius taught me, by mineral type, left to right. I did not notice I was doing it until I was done. Inside, the darkness is a gentle lie, punctuated by entire walls of phosphorescent minerals that pulse with internal light --- pinks, ochres, greens. Above, the ceilings are carpeted with constellations of glow-worms. A complete, silent world of flora thrives in this perpetual twilight. I began a binomial for the glowing moss and abandoned it mid-word; nothing down here has been described, and no journal at home would print me if I tried. The Manaïari have left their marks; a stone arch framing a particularly breathtaking grotto, a basin carved to catch dripping water, a bridge over a stream. They visit, they build, but they do not stay. They seem to understand that one does not live inside a lung; one passes through, to breathe.

Higher, much higher, hang what I have no choice but to call floating islands --- islands, floating, complete with their own mountains, plains, and underground caves (although the notion of "underground" grows slippery when one stands beneath the ground in question).

Of them, I know only what I can see and what the wind carries down. The Manaïari speak of them with a familiar reverence, these airborne quarries from which they fetch the impossible blue stone. The dragons fly up to them as casually as I would cross a road. This evening the islands turned amber, then violet, then went dark one by one as the sun left them. I watched until the highest one went out.
