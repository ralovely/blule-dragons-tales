Some dragons leave. This should not have surprised me, but it did. I had spent so long thinking of Aizomea as a closed world, a secret kept shut, that I forgot the obvious: the secret has legs. And wings. And, in some cases, flippers.

They go for all sorts of reasons, or for no reason at all. Some are curious. Some are restless. One, Manami told me, left because it didn't like the weather. The Fog-Weavers make no effort to stop them. There is a particular tilt in the way a dragon flies that signals intention, and the mist parts. There is no ceremony to it, and no permission asked. Some stay away for years. Some for centuries. One, I am told, departed roughly five hundred years ago and came back smelling faintly of olive oil. It settled into its old sleeping spot and took up where it had left off five centuries before.

The Manaïari believe dragons follow the blue stone's call across oceans, a frequency too low for human ears that carries like a pulse through the water. A lighthouse you hear rather than see. I think my compass works on the same principle. The eye that brought me here was not pointing north. It was looking.

I have spent years cataloguing dragons outside of Aizomea. Nangula spent decades before me. And before her, centuries of mythology: sea serpents in naval logs, gargoyles that turned out not to be stone, carved dragon prows on merchant ships. We knew they were real.
The whole bestiary I spent my career assembling was just the guest book of a place I hadn't found yet.

The hunting looks different from this side, too. I knew the numbers. I knew about the tail feathers for hats, the wings ground into paste, the wretched Hans the Hunter dynasty, whose sons and grandsons walked into the same jaws with the same idiotic confidence, generation after generation, stupidity being the one trait the family reliably passed down. But I had only ever seen the damage from the outside. Here I see the ones who came back. The scars are different when you recognise the animal. One returned carrying a musket ball in its shoulder, centuries old, grown over with scale like bark over a nail. It had simply kept flying. Somebody ought to do something about this. Somebody with more authority than a woman with a notebook and a loud opinion.

*(Editor's note: She did.)*
