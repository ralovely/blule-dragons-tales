The hot baths were here long before the Manaïari. Long before any human set foot on Aizomea, certain fire dragons had taken to heating pools of still water with their breath, slowly, patiently, until the surface steamed. Other dragons would gather and soak. It was, as far as I can tell, the island's oldest communal ritual, and it had nothing to do with hygiene. It was social. Dragons who would otherwise keep to themselves sat shoulder to shoulder in warm water and did nothing at all, together.

When the first settlers arrived, sometime in the fifth or sixth century, they found the pools already occupied. The Japanese among them recognised something in it. They had hot springs at home, geothermal ones, but they had never thought to sit in them together, as a practice, as a daily thing. Here the dragons had made it an institution. The settlers joined. The dragons made room.

Centuries later, if the stories are to be believed, a man of Japanese descent left Aizomea. One of the quiet returns. His family name may have been Onseki, or near enough; the oral records are old and worn at the edges. The story goes that he brought with him the idea of the communal bath house: hot water, yes, but more than that, the ritual around it. The sitting. The silence. The etiquette. I am piecing this together from fragments, and it may be wishful thinking. But I find the resemblance to the word "onsen" difficult to ignore.
