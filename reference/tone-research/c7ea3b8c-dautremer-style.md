## The Rebecca Dautremer Style Guide: A Manual for Whimsy and Wonder

### I. Core Philosophy: The Beauty in the Forgotten and the Absurd

The Dautremer style operates on a simple but profound principle: the world is far more strange, sad, and funny than traditional stories admit. It finds magic not in grand castles, but in the forgotten princesses who live there. It finds humour not in jokes, but in the deadpan description of a catastrophic life. It finds poetry in the dreams of a tiny insect.

The core of this style is to present a world that is **simultaneously familiar and utterly surreal**. It feels like a half-remembered fairy tale or a secret whispered in a dusty library. The goal is to charm the reader with inventive concepts, touch them with moments of gentle melancholy, and make them laugh at the quiet absurdity of it all.

---

### II. Tone and Mood: A Concoction of Contrasts

The overall tone is a delicate balance. It's essential to blend these elements, often within the same piece.

* **Whimsical & Playful:** The world is a place for invention. Create new words (portmanteaus like "Barbedouce"), new rules (an international fan alphabet), and new concepts (princess seeds). The text should feel like it's having fun with its own ideas.
* **Mock-Scholarly & Archival:** Frame fantastic information as if it were factual, catalogued knowledge. Use phrases like "everything we know of," "it is said that," and include footnotes, cross-references ("see page X"), and encyclopedia-style sidebars. This serious treatment of silly subjects is a primary source of humour.
* **Gently Melancholic:** There's a persistent undercurrent of sadness. Princesses are "forgotten" or "vanished." Characters yearn for escape or mourn loss ("Goodbye, my brother!"). This poignancy prevents the whimsy from becoming frivolous and gives the world emotional depth.
* **Subtly Humorous & Dry:** Avoid obvious jokes. The humour is in the understatement and the matter-of-fact delivery of bizarre information. For example, "Bothered by the little pea, the princess died the next day, from having spent a dreadful night." The catastrophe is presented calmly, making it funnier.
* **Conspiratorial & Intimate:** The narrator often speaks directly to the reader, inviting them into a secret world. Use phrases like "Do you know...?", "All that's left is for you to delve in," and "And who knows, perhaps you will recognise yourself in them?"

---

### III. Structural and Formatting Hallmarks

The structure is not linear but encyclopedic and scrapbook-like. It suggests a collection of found documents, diary entries, and scattered notes.

* **Use a Scrapbook/Encyclopedia Format:** The main narrative can be a description (like for a princess), but it must be surrounded by contextualising elements.
    * **Handwritten Epigraphs:** Start sections with a short, poetic, or philosophical phrase that appears handwritten. Examples: "To sleep is to tell oneself stories one does not yet know," or "To sulk is to push a wall that will not move."
    * **Sidebars & Asides:** Include columns or boxed text with related (but often tangential) "facts," definitions, or lists. This is a key area for world-building and humour (e.g., the puns on musical instruments, the definition of "Boudoir").
    * **Cross-Referencing:** Create the illusion of a larger, interconnected work by referencing other "pages" (e.g., "see princesses Dîng and Dông, pages 50/51").
* **Embrace Lists and Catalogues:** Lists are a powerful tool for delivering imaginative concepts quickly. Examples include the different types of wishes made at a princess's cradle or Tom Thumb's exhaustive list of nicknames.
* **Incorporate Different Narrative Forms:** Mix encyclopedic entries with first-person diary entries ("Tom Thumb") or lyrical, poetic prose ("Zazou the Cicada"). This creates a rich, layered texture.

### IV. The Linguistic Secret Sauce: How to Sound like Dautremer

This is the most crucial section. The specific use of language is what defines the style.

#### **A. Wordplay is Paramount**

* **Puns:** The language should be riddled with puns, especially those that play on double meanings. The French texts show this with *l'arme/les larmes* (weapon/tears) and *éventer* (to fan/to divulge). In English, find similar opportunities. For example, a princess who is a musician could be prone to "treble."
* **Portmanteaus & Neologisms:** Invent new words by blending existing ones. "Barbedouce" (Sweetbeard) is a perfect example. Create names for characters, places, or objects that are descriptive and inventive. Examples: Princess "Pêtsec" (Dry-fart/stiff), "Cello-winks" (violoncelle/cils).
* **Descriptive Names:** Characters are often named for their primary trait: Princess **Amnesia**, Princess **Capriciosa**, Princess **Molle** (Soft/Languid). This is a fairy-tale trope pushed to its logical, humorous extreme.

#### **B. The Power of Juxtaposition**

* **Mix the Magical with the Mundane:** This is a cornerstone of the style's humour and charm. A fairy godmother transforms a straw hat into a racing carriage; a curse is the result of a "solitary cousin" being missed on an invitation list; a princess's magical tears can be foiled by bad marks in mathematics.
* **Anachronism is Your Friend:** Place modern concepts in a fairy-tale setting. A princess can ride a bicycle with no hands, find clothes at a "thrift shop," or worry about putting on a kilo.

#### **C. Embrace Absurd & Surreal Logic**

The world must operate on its own, internally consistent but externally bizarre logic.

* **Literalise Metaphors:** If a princess is scatterbrained, make her literally "in pieces" or "higgledy-piggledy" (Princess Incomplète).
* **Create Ludicrously Specific Rules:** Develop complex systems for simple things, like the "International Fan Alphabet," where specific gestures convey entire, complex sentences.
* **Personify Concepts:** Create characters that are the living embodiment of an idea, like Princess **Fatrasie** (Jumble/Nonsense), who speaks in non-sequiturs, or Princess **Esperluette** (&), who loves words above all else.

#### **D. Master the Understated Delivery**

* **Describe Calamity Calmly:** State shocking or tragic events in a simple, matter-of-fact tone. Princess Badaboum's life is a "catastrophic life," and her suitor left after "a broken arm, a crushed hand and other niceties too long to list." This dry delivery makes the absurd content funnier.
* **Use Specific, Quirky Details:** Ground fantastic concepts with strange, precise details. A bath isn't just luxurious; it might contain "donkey's milk or apricot syrup." A stepmother doesn't just have potions; she has "prune syrup (to make you poo)" and "bean liqueur (to not make you poo)."

### V. Quick Reference: Do's and Don'ts

| Do ✅ | Don't ❌ |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Invent new words** and use puns freely. | Rely on clichés or generic fantasy tropes. |
| **Mix fairy-tale elements with modern details.** | Keep the world strictly medieval or magical. |
| **Describe absurd events in a calm, factual tone.** | Use exclamation points or overly dramatic language. |
| **Frame text like a scrapbook or encyclopedia.** | Write in a straightforward, linear narrative. |
| **Embrace gentle sadness and melancholy.** | Be overly sentimental or saccharine. |
| **Create characters who embody a single, strong concept.** | Develop complex, psychologically realistic characters. |
| **Use lists and catalogues to convey ideas.** | Write long, dense paragraphs of prose. |
| **Write poetically and lyrically for emotional moments.** | Be purely academic or dry in your tone. |

By following this guide, you can begin to craft stories and texts that capture the unique, enchanting, and wonderfully strange spirit of Rebecca Dautremer's world.
