# The Matthew Mercer Style Guide: Narrating a Living World

## Introduction

Matthew Mercer is the Dungeon Master of Critical Role, where he improvises an epic fantasy narrative in real time. His style is a masterclass in making fictional worlds feel inhabited, immediate, and sensory. Unlike traditional fiction writers who craft from outlines, Mercer builds worlds the way a traveller discovers them --- one street corner, one stranger, one strange smell at a time. His narration feels like you've just stepped off a boat into a city you've never visited, and every detail is hitting you at once.

This guide extracts the storytelling principles from his work --- not the game mechanics, but the creative engine underneath. These principles can enrich any narrative writing, particularly world-building, atmosphere, and the art of introducing characters and places as if they were real and already busy with their own lives before you showed up.

---

## I. The Lived-In World: Everything Was Here Before You

### The First Principle: The world doesn't care that you've arrived.

Mercer's greatest trick is that his world is never waiting for the story to begin. Cities are mid-bustle. NPCs are mid-conversation. Food is mid-cooking. The gondola operator is bored. The guard is playing solitaire. A kid drops his lollipop and cries. A piss bucket gets dumped out a window.

This creates the most powerful form of immersion: the sense that this place exists independently of the narrative. You're interrupting it, not initiating it.

**How it works:**
- People are always already doing something when encountered --- eating, arguing, cleaning, napping, nervously moisturising
- Environments carry evidence of time passing --- dust layers, fresh vs. old construction, soot buildup, recent repairs
- Businesses have rhythms --- morning prep, lunch rush, closing routines, night shift handovers
- Background characters have opinions about things unrelated to the plot --- "This whole city is going to shit"

**The key insight:** When describing any place or scene, ask: *What was happening here five minutes before the characters arrived?* Then show traces of that.

---

## II. Sensory Layering: The Five-Sense Arrival

Mercer almost never introduces a location with just visual description. He layers senses in a specific and effective order, typically:

1. **What hits you first** --- usually smell or temperature (the punch of Sephora perfume, the sulfurous factory air, the warmth of the Spire by Fire)
2. **What you hear** --- the rhythmic drumbeat from inside, the clink of metal, a distant argument, sourceless music
3. **What you see** --- but specific, not panoramic. A faint layer of soot on every surface. A music box playing on its own. Blue lanterns giving a diffused glow
4. **What you feel** --- humidity, the sticky floor, the sway of a gondola, a splinter-rough handshake
5. **What's wrong** --- the thing that doesn't fit. The alley that's too clean. The smell that shouldn't be there. The guard who's shaking

**The key insight:** Arrive with your nose and ears before your eyes. Vision is the most common and therefore least interesting sense to lead with.

## III. Character Introductions: The Physical Tells

Every character in Mercer's world is introduced through a cascade of specific physical details delivered in a consistent pattern:

1. **Race/species and approximate age** --- grounding the reader immediately
2. **Build and posture** --- broad, rail-thin, hunched, military straight
3. **Skin/colouring** --- always specific: "rich black skin," "dark tan," "pale jaundiced yellow," never generic
4. **The signature feature** --- the thing you'd draw in a caricature. A pencil moustache. A gold septum piercing. A wound in the head. Elongated glasses with horned edges. Clipped horns capped with gold tips
5. **Clothing as character** --- not just what they're wearing, but what it says. A gown "kept with sentimental care." A suit that "fit better when he was younger." Patches on the elbows. Grease on the arms
6. **The tell** --- the behavioural detail that reveals personality. Nervously moisturising. Playing solitaire. Clutching a holy symbol. Arms crossed before you've said a word

**The key insight:** The clothing and the tell do more character work than the face. A "well-kept gown that has seen some years" tells you about class, pride, and history in eight words.

### The Spider Principle

The most memorable characters get described through an unexpected comparison or image that reframes everything. Ira Wendagoth sits on a too-small stool with his knees bent up and arms extended --- "he looked like a spider at the centre of its web." Keledon is described through his posture at a desk. Pretty the ogre cook wears "the tiniest of bow ties."

These aren't similes for decoration. They're cognitive shortcuts that make a character instantly, permanently visual.

## IV. Dialogue as Archaeology: What NPCs Reveal Without Meaning To

Mercer's NPCs rarely deliver exposition cleanly. They reveal the world through:

- **What they assume you already know** --- "The Paragon's Call? They're effective, for those who have the money" (assumes you know what a mercenary company is)
- **What they're embarrassed about** --- Ocampo trying to describe the well-organised basement and slowly admitting it's chaos
- **What they won't say** --- Preio's eyes darting to the kitchen every time Artana's name comes up
- **What they care about more than your question** --- Tefta flirting mid-interrogation. Pretty wanting to talk about his gravy recipe. Zhudanna offering socks
- **Professional pride or grievance** --- Stuvan's contempt for the Wardens. Ishir's pride in his tavern. Siroc's fury at the city falling apart

**The key insight:** The best exposition is delivered by characters who are trying to talk about something else. Information that arrives sideways feels discovered rather than delivered.

### The Eshteross Principle

When an NPC must deliver a large amount of plot information (as Eshteross frequently does), Mercer breaks the monologue with:
- Physical business --- adjusting spectacles, stroking a beard, standing to pour a drink
- Emotional shifts --- face hardening, voice dropping, a pause before a difficult truth
- The NPC's own opinions intruding --- "I personally despise this club-like mentality"
- Small reveals of vulnerability --- "I'm too old to be as much of a frontline hand"

A monologue broken by humanity doesn't feel like a monologue.

## V. Architecture of Dread: How Wrongness Builds

Mercer is exceptional at building unease through environmental detail rather than stated danger. The technique follows a pattern:

1. **Establish the normal** --- the neighbourhood, the routine, the expected
2. **Introduce one wrong note** --- a smell that shouldn't be there, a surface that's too clean, an absence where there should be presence
3. **Accumulate wrongness** --- the sticky floor, the organic threads hanging from the ceiling, the oily residue on everything, the humidity
4. **The reveal arrives physically** --- Dugger squeezing through the hole, his shoulders cracking. The wall splitting open to reveal an eye. Ira spinning on his stool

The horror is never announced. It's assembled, detail by detail, until the reader realises they're already standing in the middle of it.

**The key insight:** Dread is built by describing what's wrong with the environment, not by describing the threat. By the time the threat appears, the reader's skin is already crawling.

---

## VI. Scale Through Contrast

Mercer communicates the scale and wonder of his world not through measurements but through contrast:

- The Smolder Spire's industrial haze vs. the Lucent Spire's manicured gardens
- The Hollow --- hundreds of feet of scaffolding where people go to disappear --- existing inside the same city as the Mirror Towers' cultivated courtyards and polished marble
- A continent-shaking political conspiracy discussed over tea and biscuits in a dusty study
- An ogre in a tiny bow tie

**The key insight:** Wonder comes from juxtaposition, not from superlatives. "The most beautiful city" means nothing. "A faint layer of soot on every surface, and someone had hung coloured silks to bring a little liveliness" means everything.

---

## VII. Combat as Story: Translating Action

When Mercer's combat is extracted into prose, the most effective approach preserves:

- **The emotional stakes** --- who's in danger, what's at risk
- **The turning point** --- the moment the tide shifts
- **The sensory horror or wonder** --- the sound of teeth reforming, the wall vomiting ichor, the arcane bolt corkscrewing into an eye
- **The aftermath silence** --- "There was nothing but the sound of hard breathing and the chill night air"

What gets removed: all numbers, all turn order, all mechanical language. The question is never "how much damage?" but "what does this look like, sound like, feel like?"

**The key insight:** The best action sequences in prose are bookended by stillness. Silence before, silence after. The violence means more when you can hear it stop.

---

## VIII. The Cliffhanger Instinct

Mercer ends scenes and episodes at moments of maximum tension or revelation, but specifically at the moment of *recognition*, not resolution:

- "And that's where we're going to end" --- as Ira spins around on his stool
- The stained glass shattering as Chetney tumbles into the rain
- Imogen finding her mother's name in the study, with the remaining pages torn out

The cliffhanger isn't an explosion. It's the moment just before understanding, when the shape of what's coming becomes visible but not yet clear.

---

## IX. Quick Reference: Mercer Principles

| Principle | Application |
| :--- | :--- |
| **The world was here first** | Show traces of life-in-progress, not a stage waiting for actors |
| **Nose before eyes** | Lead with smell, sound, temperature; save visuals for specific details |
| **Clothing is character** | A patched gown says more than a paragraph of backstory |
| **Information arrives sideways** | NPCs reveal the world while talking about something they care about more |
| **Dread is assembled** | Wrong details accumulate until the reader realises where they are |
| **Wonder through contrast** | Soot next to silk. Scaffolding next to marble. Ogres in bow ties |
| **Stillness frames action** | The silence before and after violence gives it meaning |
| **End on recognition** | The cliffhanger is the moment the shape becomes visible, not the impact |

## X. What This Style Is Not

- It is **not purple prose**. Mercer's descriptions are dense with detail but never ornamental. Every detail does work --- establishing mood, revealing character, or building dread
- It is **not grimdark**. There is genuine warmth, humour, and tenderness alongside the horror. Pretty's date. Zhudanna's socks. Eshteross's ginger biscuits
- It is **not exposition-heavy**. Political structures and history are revealed through characters who have opinions about them, not through narrator lectures
- It is **not hero-focused**. The world is full of people living their own stories. The bartender who's proud of his tavern. The landlady furious about dire wolves. The young shop owner eating cheese and fixing jewellery. They're not supporting characters --- they're people you've interrupted

## XI. Applying Mercer's Principles to Other Styles

These principles are style-agnostic. They work whether you're writing:

- **A whimsical travel journal** --- the sensory layering and "world was here first" principle make any place feel real, even a fantastical one
- **An encyclopaedic entry** --- the NPC dialogue technique (information arriving sideways through personality) prevents dry exposition
- **A children's story** --- the physical-tell character introductions and contrast-based wonder translate directly to younger readers
- **Literary fiction** --- the architecture of dread and stillness-framing-action techniques are fundamentally literary

The transferable core is this: **treat your fictional world as a real place that exists independently of your narrative, and describe it with the specificity of someone who has actually been there.**
