Aizomea - The Land of Dragons
=============================

## 1. Where the World Keeps Its Secrets [T1]

Here I am, scribbling away in my journal. The scent is a peculiar mix of damp earth and something distinctly... draconic. Not fiery, mind you, but warm and ancient, like sunlight on old stone. If you were to ask me exactly where I am, Cassius, I'd have to wave my hands vaguely towards the great, shimmering expanse of the Pacific Ocean.

Imagine drawing a curled-up serpent on a map, somewhere between New Zealand and South America. Then, imagine it as a brand new landmass with secrets tucked into every fold. That's Aizomea for you, a place that delights in being just beyond definition, yet utterly, undeniably, here.

Its edges are never quite the same from one day to the next, dissolving into a pearl-grey mist that smells of salt and eucalyptus haze, only to reform into a jagged coastline of deep blue cliffs. From above, the whole thing resembles a contented, slumbering dragon --- its spine a range of cobalt mountains, its belly a patchwork of plains and sparkling rivers. I've tried to sketch it twice now. Both times it had moved by the time I put my pencil down. A continent with no interest in being mapped.

And yet, when I feel the soft moss beneath my feet and hear the distant, happy rumble of a sleeping dragon, I know it's the most real place I've ever been.

*Day twelve.*


## 2. First Days [T1]

*Day three.*

I am writing this because if I do not write it down, I will convince myself it is not real. I am a scientist. The notebook is the only tool I have that still works.

I woke three days ago in a room I did not recognise. Stone ceiling. A bed too large for me, built for someone broader, taller. The air smelled of wet stone and something warm I had no name for. Something was breathing on the other side of the wall. Slowly, deeply, on a scale I could feel through the floor.

I was outside within the hour. A dragon was sleeping in the path. Just lying there, the way a dog lies in a doorway. I stood in my doorframe, heart hammering, until a child walked up, stepped over the dragon's tail without looking down, and disappeared around a corner. So I stepped over it too. The village was behind it. Full of people, full of noise, full of life that had been happening long before I arrived and showed no sign of adjusting to my presence. Everything is built at two scales: human doors beside dragon-wide arches, narrow stairs next to broad ramps, as if two villages had been shuffled together like a deck of cards. Even the human scale is too large for me. The doorframes are generous enough that I walk through them feeling like a child visiting a grown-up's house. I was furniture delivered to the wrong address.

A woman found me standing in the middle of a path, staring. She introduced herself as Manami, in slow but clear English, and handed me a bowl of something warm. She did not ask if I was all right. She told me where the water was, where I could wash, and that nobody here would hurt me. When I said Nangula's name, she went quiet for a moment, then nodded, as if that explained why I was here. She sat with me while I ate. It was the best thing I have ever tasted. My mother would have understood this meal. I would like to say I received this with dignity.

Three days in, and I am only beginning to understand where I am. There are dragons everywhere. Not one or two, glimpsed at a distance, the way the rest of the world has always known them. Hundreds. Living alongside humans as if this were the most ordinary arrangement in the world. They sleep in the paths and the children climb over them. They eat beside the cooking fires. A pair of them were arguing over a sunny spot on a rock this morning with the petty energy of two cats disputing a windowsill. Everything I thought I knew about dragons was built on fragments. This is the whole picture, and it is nothing like I imagined.


## 3. The Manaïari [T1]

One does not argue with a mountain, and the Manaïari seem to have built their entire philosophy on this simple, irrefutable fact. When one shares a home with dragons, the petty squabbles of humankind must seem rather small. Two men were disagreeing loudly over the placement of a fishing net this morning. A dragon yawned --- a real yawn, the kind that rearranges the wind --- and both men looked at each other, shrugged, and went for lunch. Problem solved.

Manami, the woman assigned to show me around (or, more likely, to keep me from wandering into something I shouldn't), is a head taller than me, and I am not short. She has the kind of shoulders that make doorframes seem like a suggestion. The first morning, she handed me a basket of fruit to carry. I took it with both hands. She'd been holding it in one, while also holding her daughter, Hana. I saw her later that afternoon at the docks, directing a fishing crew with the same calm she uses on me. She is not my guide. She has a life. I am, at best, an addition to it.

Everyone I have met so far is taller than me. Broader. I have never felt so frightfully medium in my life. A woman carried a beam across the square yesterday on one shoulder, talking to her neighbour as she went. A man was shaping a plank with his bare hands, working the wood as if it were clay. Their teeth are extraordinary --- white, straight, complete, from the children to the eldest. I thought of every dentist's waiting room I have ever sat in and felt a pang of national shame. The women move with a settled authority, as if leadership were so old here it had stopped being a subject. The men check with them before they act, the way a ship checks the wind. The whole place has an ease to it that is the opposite of London. Closer, perhaps, to the south of France, where people also eat slowly and argue beautifully. There are no fences here. Anywhere. I keep looking for property boundaries and finding none.

Their defining trait, however, is an unwavering commitment to living well. They are, in the truest sense, *bon vivants*. Every problem, big or small, is best solved over a shared meal, and their talent for food borders on genius. Every fruit, every spice is understood and celebrated, combined into something that makes you close your eyes and forget your own name. I have eaten things here I cannot spell, and I have gone back for seconds every time. They eat well, and it shows. I have not seen a single person here who looks unwell. I do, however, miss tea. Proper tea. I have not given up hope. I wonder if Cassius is remembering to water the garden.

They do not worship nature, nor try to conquer it. They cook with it, build with it, sing with it --- and the land, from what I can tell, leans in too.

This appetite for life extends, naturally, to the dragons. The bond between them is not one of master and beast, nor even of friends, but of family --- a sprawling, chaotic family where scales sit beside fur, feathers beside skin. They do not try to tame the wild. They lean in and listen, the way one listens to a very old, very wise relative who speaks softly, never repeats herself, and is known to bite.

*Day nineteen.*


## 4. Language [T1]

*Day twenty-eight.*

The language here sounds like water over stones. Lots of vowels, soft consonants, a rhythm that rises and falls without ever quite hurrying. It is, I think, a language designed to be overheard by sleeping dragons. There is even a formal register, spoken more softly and slowly, used only near dragons, out of respect for the Listeners who might be working nearby.

I am not starting from nothing. Manami speaks English, learnt from Nangula over her many visits, and a handful of others know enough to help me when I point at something and look lost. I am teaching Manami more in return, and she is a quicker student than I am. This is not a compliment to her so much as a confession about me.

I have been saying what I thought meant "good morning" for three weeks now. Manami told me yesterday that I have in fact been saying something closer to "the fish agrees." She waited three weeks. She says it was funnier that way. The children, who have been following me around repeating everything I say, have apparently known all along. They are still saying it. To each other. While looking at me.

The grammar is its own world. The verb "to be" does not work the way I expect. You do not say "I am hungry." You say something closer to "hunger visits me." Emotions, states of mind, even weather --- they are all visitors. They arrive. They stay. They leave. You do not own your sadness. It is passing through. I find this either profound or maddening, depending on the day.

There are things in this language that English cannot hold. There is no word for "own" or "possess." You can say "I use this" or "this stays with me" but not "this is mine." There is a tense that does not exist in English, something between past and present, used for things that happened but whose effects are still felt. Not "the dragon left" but something closer to "the dragon left and the leaving is still here." They use it for grief. For seasons. For love. I have no way to translate it. I have started using it anyway.

Some words are borrowed directly from dragon sounds. A low rumble that means something like "contentment" has entered the human vocabulary. People use it in conversation, dropping their voice into a register that is not quite speech and not quite growl. The first time I heard it, I thought the man speaking had a stomach complaint. He was telling someone he loved his house.

There is no written form. Everything is oral. I may be the first person to write Manaïari words down, scratching them into the margins of a notebook in a spelling system I am inventing as I go. I feel guilty about this sometimes, as if I am pinning butterflies.

Place names change. A hill is not named. It is called "the hill where the old dragon slept" until a new dragon sleeps there, and then it becomes something else. A path is "the path past the red flowers" until the flowers die, and then it is "the path where the red flowers were." The landscape is a living sentence, constantly being revised. I asked Manami for directions last week and she sent me to a landmark that had, apparently, been renamed the previous afternoon. I arrived somewhere else entirely. She did not see the problem. I have since discovered that the paths themselves are more reliable than their names. The stone stays warm where people have walked on it. The more a route is used, the warmer it gets. The path to the communal kitchen is almost hot. A path nobody has walked in weeks is cold. I can find my way home in the dark by the warmth under my soles.

The word for "blue" and the word for "home" are nearly identical. The tonal difference is so slight I cannot hear it. I keep saying one when I mean the other. Nobody corrects me. I am beginning to suspect that, here, it may not matter which one I mean.

I am learning this language the way one learns to swim: badly, in public, swallowing a great deal of water. Being bilingual helps --- the French half of my brain is more willing to bend than the English half, which keeps insisting on rules. But something is shifting. I catch myself thinking in it sometimes, just for a moment, before the English rushes back in. In those moments, the world here makes a kind of sense it does not make in my own tongue. I am not yet fluent. I may never be. But I am starting to hear the shape of what I am missing.


## 5. Whispers of the Heart [T1]

This morning I watched a woman sit with her hand flat against a dragon's flank for the better part of an hour. Neither moved. The dragon's breathing was slow and deep, warm enough to shift the grass. When the woman finally stood, she walked straight to the village well, lowered a bucket, and brought it back. The dragon drank. Nobody had said a word.

Dragons do not speak. I need to write that down plainly, because I spent my first months here waiting for them to. I talked to dozens, in English, in French, in what little Manaïari I've picked up. They listened. They always listen. Their eyes follow your sentences the way a cat follows a bird, and you'd swear on your life they understand every syllable. But they never answer. Not in language. Not once.

The woman at the dragon's flank is what the Manaïari call a Listener. There are perhaps thirty in the village, women mostly, though not all. You can spot them by their hands: the palms are always warm. Not metaphorically. Warm to the touch, even on cold mornings, as if they carry a small fire beneath the skin. A Listener places her hand on a dragon and goes still. When she rises, she knows things. Not words. Not sentences. Something closer to weather, a shift in pressure or warmth. I asked one what it was like. She thought for a long time. "Like hearing colour," she said, which clarified nothing and somehow everything.

Manami arranged for me to try. An old, patient dragon who apparently tolerates foreigners. I placed my palm on its side. Warm scales. I closed my eyes. I concentrated fiercely. I felt... a heartbeat. The vague sense of being near something very large and very calm. Nothing else. After twenty minutes, the dragon opened one eye, looked at me, and exhaled slowly through its nostrils. Even I could read that one. I said "sorry" before I could stop myself. (I have been saying "sorry" to everything on this island. Trees, doorframes, a rock I tripped over. The British affliction travels.) Manami was already laughing. "You think too loudly," she said. I wonder if Nangula was better at this. I suspect she was. She was better at most things that required silence.

But what fascinates me most is not what the dragons express. It's what they withhold. The Manaïari language contains fourteen separate words for different qualities of dragon silence. Fourteen. I have managed to learn seven. There is a word for the silence of a dragon that is resting, and a different word for the silence of a dragon that is pretending to rest. A word for the silence before a dragon acts, and another for the silence after it has decided not to. Manami tried to explain the remaining seven. She could not. "They can only be felt," she said. "Like the difference between two blues." She saw me looking at my fingertips (I still don't know why they've turned faintly blue) and smiled, as if that were answer enough.


## 6. Sound Instruments [T1]

The Listeners use touch. But there is a second tradition, older and more public, that uses sound.

The instruments are simple. Hollowed seashells that produce a single clear note. Smooth river stones clicked together in rhythms. Feathers drawn across taut string to make a hum so faint I can barely hear it, though the dragons hear it from across a valley. And gourds, grown from seeds the Japanese settlers carried with them, dried and filled with seeds or pebbles or, in the rarest cases, blue stone dust. A gourd filled with blue stone dust sounds like the voice of the stone, bottled.

Each instrument reaches a different kind of dragon. The seashell carries over water. The stones travel through rock into caves. The feather-hum is for the small, shy species that flee from anything louder. Conversation is the wrong word. A fisherman blows a shell and a sea dragon changes course. A child clicks stones at a cave entrance and a dragon inside shifts to make room. Call and response, but the response is movement, not sound.

I tried the seashell. The note I produced was, I was told, technically correct. The sea dragon surfaced, looked at me, and submerged again. Manami took the shell, blew what sounded like the same note, and the dragon came alongside the boat. "It's not the note," she said. "It's the breath behind it."

One evening I heard a gourd played at dusk, a low rattling sequence I had not heard before. Three dragons wheeling high above the valley turned and came back to the village. I asked what the sequence meant. "Dinner," someone said.


## 7. Underground Labyrinths [T1]

The landscape of Aizomea is a bit otherworldly, not really following any geological pattern I was taught at university. It's like something has pulled the mountains up, like a child who's grown too much, too quickly. The mountains are tall but not menacing. They leave space for light to cover the valleys where most of the Manaïari live. Some of the ridges have an odd quality to them. Curves that look almost deliberate. I sketched one this morning that, from a certain angle, resembled a sleeping animal. Probably a coincidence.

The mountains are also home to gigantic caves. The climb to reach them is beastly, but one enters through tunnels worn smooth, probably by the passage of dragons over centuries, and forgets the legs immediately. The scale is humbling. I caught myself arranging my sketches the way Cassius taught me, by mineral type, left to right. I did not notice I was doing it until I was done. Inside, the darkness is a gentle lie, punctuated by entire walls of phosphorescent minerals that pulse with a soft, internal light---pinks, ochres, and soft greens. Above, the ceilings are carpeted with constellations of glow-worms. A complete, silent world of flora thrives in this perpetual twilight. The Manaïari have left their marks; a stone arch framing a particularly breathtaking grotto, a basin carved to catch dripping water, a bridge over a stream. They visit, they build, but they do not stay. It is as if they understand one does not live inside a lung; one passes through to breathe.

Higher, much higher, are what can only be described as floating islands that have untethered themselves from the world, complete with their own set of mountains, plains, and underground caves (although the very notion of being above or below ground is challenging when one stands up there).

Of it, I know only what I can see and what the wind carries down. The Manaïari speak of them with a familiar reverence, these airborne quarries from which they fetch the impossible blue stone. I watch the dragons fly to a land I cannot map, a delightful, geological absurdity that hangs just out of my reach, and I feel like a child staring at a jar of sweets on the highest shelf.

## 9. The Blue Stone's Kiss [T1]

It started with a colour. A blue so deep and resonant it seemed to hum. I first noticed it in the mountains --- impossibly tall, thin spires, as if something had pulled them upward by their tips. At their base, the rock is pale, ordinary granite. But the higher one looks, the deeper the blue becomes, until the very peaks are a vivid ultramarine, stained by something no longer quite there. Whatever gave them that colour has long since risen further, into the sky. The mountains are just the memory of it, stretched thin and left behind.

But the blue is not only in the rock. It is everywhere.

It's in the subtle, iridescent sheen of the seasoning a Manaïari child playfully sprinkled on a sleeping dragon's snout. It's in the soft glow of the pendants worn by everyone, a quiet, personal star against their skin. I've seen it in the glint of a cooking pot, or in the thimbles of seamstresses stitching festival cloaks with shed dragon scales.

This blue is more than a pigment; it's a presence. A stone, they tell me, a collaboration between the sky-borne dragons and the earth-bound Manaïari. It is both mundane and magical.

I find myself looking for it everywhere now, this thread of blue that stitches a world together. I have a hundred questions. It just sits there, vibrating faintly. I keep coming back to the colour. I have written about it three times now and each time it sounds wrong. The rock I brought to Prague, the one that hummed at dusk, the one Nangula recognised before I did. It was this. It was always this.

One of the harvesters let me hold a raw stone today. It was heavier than it looked, and cool --- noticeably cool, as if it carried a memory of altitude. When I gave it back, my fingertips were blue. I licked my thumb and rubbed. Nothing. Manami laughed. She held up her own hands, palms out, a faint, permanent blue soaked into the skin like a dye that had forgotten how to leave. All the harvesters have them, she said. You can spot one across a crowded market by the colour of her hands. It doesn't wash off. It isn't meant to.

I spoke with them afterwards. They were just back, their faces flushed from the wind and altitude, carrying baskets filled with what looked like ordinary, dull blue rocks. It is only when you hold one that you feel a faint, latent vibration. I have taken three samples. One for the laboratory. Two because Cendre collects rocks and I cannot help myself.

They tell me the true source of the stone lies within the floating islands that graze the sky. Deep inside caves hidden beneath the mountains that crown these airborne lands. Reaching them requires a dragon. In fact, this harvest is the only accepted reason for a human ever to ride one.

The partnership is born of necessity. A dragon's power to fly to these secluded caves, a human's gentle hand to prise the fragile stones from the earth without shattering them. The riders, all women, sing on the way up. I can hear them from the ground, fading as they climb. They speak of the journey with a quiet awe, of the silence in the clouds and the cool, dark peace of the caves.

I should say clearly: this is the only reason a human ever rides a dragon. The only one. I have asked. I have hinted. I have, on one occasion, stood beside a particularly obliging-looking dragon and tried to look light. Nobody rides for pleasure, for transport, for sport, for war. A dragon is not a horse. It is not waiting to be mounted. A dragon carries a human when the dragon sees a reason to. The harvest is that reason. There are no others.

They would not take me with them. It is a rite I am not privy to. I can only stand here on solid ground, examine the rough-hewn treasures they've brought back, and try to imagine the view from a dragon's back on the way to a mountain in the sky. It is a queer feeling to be so close to the story, yet to be missing the opening chapter.


## 10. Gold Means Nothing Here [T1]

I found gold in the riverbed on my fifth day. Not flakes. Chunks. I picked one up. It was the size of my fist. I stood there holding it, heart pounding, until a child walked past, looked at me, looked at the rock, and kept walking with the expression of someone who has just watched an adult do something pointless.

There is gold everywhere. The paths in some parts of the village are paved with it, because it was the flattest stone available when a stretch got muddy. I noticed it in the walls of the communal kitchen, pressed into the gaps between stones as mortar. The children use nuggets as game tokens. I watched a girl flick a piece worth a small English house off the edge of a terrace and into the grass below. Nobody went to look for it.

I asked Manami about gold. She looked puzzled. "The yellow stone?" she said. "It's soft. It bends. What would you do with it?" I opened my mouth to explain the entire global economy, then closed it again.

There is no money here. I have been trying to understand how anything gets done without it, and the answer appears to be: easily. People give what they have. They take what they need. Big tasks are done together. Small things flow without being counted. Favours are remembered but not tracked. I asked who enforces fairness. Manami looked puzzled again, which is her way of telling me I have asked the wrong question. I suppose when you live alongside creatures who could take anything they wanted and choose not to, generosity stops being a virtue and becomes the only arrangement that makes sense.

I pocketed a few pieces that evening. I am not proud of this (except that I am, a little). I do not yet know how or when I will be going home, or even if the journey back is mine to decide. But if I do make it back to England, the contents of my coat pocket would pay for the roof repairs, Cendre's school fees, and enough funding to keep my research going for a year. Here, I had just stolen a handful of gravel. I may be the first person in history to feel guilty about picking up rocks from a riverbed.

## 12. Daily Life [T3]

Nothing of note happened today. No hatchings. No ceremonies. No dragons did anything I haven't seen before. I had breakfast, walked to the ridge, sat in the sun, talked with the neighbours, ate something I cannot spell the name of, watched the stars come out. A quiet day. Perhaps the best kind. So I thought I might write about the ordinary instead, since the ordinary here is extraordinary enough.

I sleep in a small stone house at the edge of the village. It was built for someone taller. The doorframe is generous. The bed is generous. Everything here is generous. The roof is flat, which I have learnt means "dragons may land." A small dragon has taken to sleeping on mine. I did not invite it. It arrived one night and stayed. It snores. I have named it Gerald.

My desk is a single slab of blue-veined stone, slightly too tall for me. Everything here is slightly too tall for me. I write by candlelight and the glow of the blue stone in the walls. My watch stopped months ago. I have not fixed it. Time here runs on dragon rhythms. The morning brushing. The afternoon soaking. The evening settling. I know what hour it is by which dragons are awake.

I arrived in sensible English field-wear. Linen shirt, canvas trousers, good boots. I now wear Manaïari wraps because they are cooler, easier, and I stopped caring about buttons somewhere in the third month. The boots I have kept. A woman must draw the line somewhere. I wash my own clothes in the river. A dragon once tried to help by breathing warm air on the wet cloth. It caught fire. We do not speak of it.

There is a rock near the edge of the ridge where I go to write and sketch. A dragon sat on it once while I was away, and when I came back it was warm. For hours. It is warm every time now. The dragon has never acknowledged me, but the rock is always ready.

Small objects have been appearing on my desk overnight. A pebble. A feather. A piece of shell. I have never seen who leaves them. Gerald is the obvious suspect, but Gerald does not move. The objects have no pattern I can identify. I keep them in a row along the windowsill. The row is getting longer.

I keep a small pot of blue stone poultice on my desk. I use it on cuts, blisters, sore joints. It has become ordinary, the way aspirin is ordinary back home.

The evenings are long and never quiet. Communal meals that stretch past dark, voices and firelight, dragons settling into their spots around the edges. By the time I get back to my desk the candle is half gone and my notes from the day are already blurring into the next meal, the next conversation, the next thing someone wants to show me. I write and sketch until my eyes ache, then sit outside and listen. I ran out of paint weeks ago. I have been making my own from what I can find. Crushed berries, charcoal, clay from the riverbed. The blue stone, ground fine, produces a pigment so vivid it makes everything else on the page look timid. My illustrations are slowly turning blue. I do not mind. The island is never silent. It is never loud either. I should also write about the birds here. Tomorrow.


## 13. Feasts and Fun [T2]

To the Manaïari, a shared meal can solve almost anything. A diplomatic incident, a broken heart, a philosophical impasse---all are best addressed around a hearth. Their entire culture seems to be a grand, joyful conspiracy of food. Harvesting it, preparing it, serving it.

I visited a restaurant yesterday with Manami, carved directly into a cliff face. She ordered for both of us without asking. Mon Dieu, the food! Humans occupied the terraces while their dragon companions perched on wider ledges below, their magnificent heads rising up to become part of the dinner party. The heat from the dragons rose gently through the stone, warming the terraces from below, and conversation bounced off the rock walls in a cheerful, incomprehensible roar. The kitchen behind it was communal, enormous, with stone counters at two heights. Even the lower one is built for people taller than me.

The menu is a wonderful kind of madness. I was served a dish of rice and phosphorescent moss, cooked on a vast, hot stone by a man who conducted the whole operation with the focus of a surgeon. When the moment came, he raised a hand, and the dragon behind him exhaled a gentle, warm breath that seared the moss, causing it to glow with a final, brilliant pulse. Neither of them needed to look at the other. It tastes of warm soil and starlight.

Then there are the berries. An ice dragon flash-freezes them with a puff of frigid air, creating a confection that is impossibly frozen on the outside with a molten, sun-hot core. A paradox on a spoon. There is also a seasoning made from dragon venom --- a few drops only --- whose flavour I can only describe as tasting of the colour yellow. I am aware that makes no sense. It is accurate nonetheless.

By the end of the evening, a dragon had fallen asleep with its chin on a terrace railing, and a child was using its lower lip as a shelf for her dessert bowl. Nobody seemed to find this remarkable.


## 14. On the Origin of Hot Baths

The hot baths were here long before the Manaïari. Long before any human set foot on Aizomea, certain fire dragons had taken to heating pools of still water with their breath, slowly, patiently, until the surface steamed. Other dragons would gather and soak. It was, as far as I can tell, the island's oldest communal ritual, and it had nothing to do with hygiene. It was social. Dragons who would otherwise keep to themselves sat shoulder to shoulder in warm water and did nothing at all, together.

When the first settlers arrived, sometime in the fifth or sixth century, they found the pools already occupied. The Japanese among them recognised something in it. They had hot springs at home, geothermal ones, but they had never thought to sit in them together, as a practice, as a daily thing. Here the dragons had made it an institution. The settlers joined. The dragons made room.

Centuries later, if the stories are to be believed, a man of Japanese descent left Aizomea. One of the quiet returns. His family name may have been something close to Onseki, though the oral records are old and worn at the edges. The story goes that he brought with him the idea of the communal bath house: hot water, yes, but more than that, the ritual around it. The sitting. The silence. The etiquette. I am piecing this together from fragments, and it may be wishful thinking. But I find the resemblance to the word "onsen" difficult to ignore.

## 15. Notes from the Pools

There are rules. Nobody wrote them down, but everyone knows them. You do not splash. You do not enter a pool already occupied by a sleeping dragon. You do not, under any circumstances, comment on the noises a dragon makes when it first lowers itself into hot water. I will not attempt to describe these noises. I will say only that They are prolonged, involuntary, and I will leave it at that, and that every dragon, without exception, looks mildly embarrassed afterwards. The Manaïari pretend not to hear.

The Manaïari have their own pools, smaller, heated by the same fire dragons. The dragons who tend these pools treat the task with a care that surprised me. One tested the water with the tip of its tail before a group of children climbed in, adjusting the temperature with a breath so gentle it barely stirred the surface. It held that temperature for an hour, perfectly steady, watching the children with one half-closed eye. Nobody thanked it. Nobody needed to.

In the warm pools on the eastern slopes, there are critters. I don't know what else to call them. They are translucent, roughly the size of a fist, and they drift on the surface like soap bubbles with nowhere to be. When popped, they release a puff of gas that smells of strawberries and affects
dragons the way a good joke makes a child laugh: instantly, totally, without any dignity whatsoever. Certain dragons will sit in a pool for hours, popping them one after another with the glazed focus of someone shelling peanuts. Pop, giggle, reach, pop, giggle, reach. A creature the size of a house, reduced to helpless wheezing by a soap bubble. The Manaïari children find this spectacle funnier than the critters themselves. I cannot blame them.

The children have also worked out that if you throw a critter at a dragon, it pops on impact and the dragon laughs whether it wanted to or not. This has become a game. The dragons pretend to be furious, but the performance is unconvincing. One dragon keeps positioning itself suspiciously close to the children's throwing range, facing the other way, tail conveniently still. It is fooling no one.


## 16. Gerald [T3]

Gerald update. He slept on the roof again last night. He has not moved since yesterday afternoon. I do not know what he eats. I have never seen him eat. I left a piece of fish near the door this morning as an experiment. When I came back, the fish was gone and Gerald had not moved. Either he is faster than he looks, or something else ate it. He did not acknowledge me. He has never acknowledged me. I am beginning to take it personally.

There is also a dragon I keep seeing asleep in different places. By the river this morning. At the market yesterday. On a rooftop the day before. Always the same dragon. Always asleep. Always somewhere new. I have never seen it move. I have never seen it awake. I am beginning to wonder if it is the same dragon at all, or if I have fundamentally misunderstood something.

The woman next door has been explaining to a dragon, at length and with considerable passion, why the cooking pot should be moved to the other side of the fire. The dragon is asleep. She does not appear to notice, or care. This happens constantly. The Manaïari hold entire conversations with dragons who are visibly not paying attention, interpreting a yawn as disagreement and a tail-flick as consent. They know perfectly well that dragons do not speak. They talk to them anyway.

Once a month, a dragon walks backwards through the village. Always the same route. Always the same time of day. The Manaïari step aside for it without looking up, the way one steps aside for a passing cart. It walks with perfect confidence, never bumping into anything, following a path it seems to know by heart. I have asked about it twice. Both times I received the kind of shrug that means the question is wrong, not the answer.

They are also, I am learning, spectacularly stubborn. There is a woman in the village who has refused to eat a certain fruit since she was nine years old. Nobody remembers why, including her. There is a man who sits in the same spot by the river every afternoon because someone once told him the sunset was good from there. The sunset is better from the ridge. He knows this. He sits there anyway.


## 17. The Matriarchs [T3]

**On Mending**

They have no courts of law here, no bewigged judges or stern-faced constables. When a dispute arises, it is not a case to be won, but a tear to be mended. The task falls to certain women, known for little more than their patience and their skill with a teapot. (I say teapot. It is in fact a shallow clay bowl of something thick, spiced, and savoury that no self-respecting Englishwoman would call tea. I call it tea. The ritual is the same.) They bring the aggrieved parties together not to argue, but to do. To repair a sail, to bake bread, to re-sole a perfectly good shoe that didn't need re-soling.
I watched one such mending yesterday. Two families, furious over a tree whose shade fell on the wrong garden, sat across from each other in stony silence. The mending woman set them to making honey cakes together. By the time the dough needed kneading, they were arguing about whether to add ginger. By the time the cakes came out, the tree had been forgotten entirely. They were now arguing about ginger. She seemed to consider this progress.
Justice here is not a sword, but a thimble. 

**On Story-Weaving**

History in Aizomea is not written in books. It is woven. In a round, sun-drenched building at the heart of the village, the great tapestries hang from floor to ceiling. I grew up visiting the tapestries at Bayeux and Angers with my mother. Those told stories of conquest. These tell stories of weather, harvest, and the passage of dragons. Here are no dates, no dry accounts of kings and battles. Instead, there is the story of a great drought told in countless shades of brown thread, the joy of a good harvest in a burst of gold and green. The women who weave them are the island's memory. The men, I later learned, are its palette. They prepare the dyes, coaxing colours from bark, roots, crushed shells --- things I could not begin to identify. To learn of the past, one does not read; one runs a hand over the heavy weave of a dragon's passing, the soft fuzz of a hatching.

I spent an afternoon there, running my fingers along decades. One tapestry near the door has a curious bald patch in its centre, conspicuously re-woven in thread that doesn't quite match. I asked the weaver about it. She said it recorded an event the village had collectively decided to be embarrassed about, and had voted to unpick. She would not tell me what it was. The thread, she said, remembers even if they'd rather it didn't.

**Village Sketches**

- The Docks. The harbour is carved into a natural cove, the stone worn smooth by centuries. The boats are small and light, designed to be hauled by one person. No need for large vessels here - they never go far. The harbour master is a young woman who directs the entire fishing fleet with a small wooden flute. No shouting, no bells. A high trill sent one boat to the eastern quay; a low, warbling tune told another its nets needed repair. The whole affair was as quiet and orderly as a well-conducted orchestra. One boat came in late. She played a single, flat note. It sounded exactly like disappointment.

- The Marketplace. Two merchants, red-faced and puffing out their chests, were arguing over a patch of shade. A woman walking past with a child remarked, loud enough for them to hear, "It reminds one of the Tale of the Two Grumpy Beetles." Then she was gone. The men deflated as if pricked by a pin, and silently sorted out their wares. The most effective weapon here, it seems, is a well-aimed fable.

- The Stonemason's. Watched a master mason at work. Her apprentice, a strong young man, could not get a great lintel to sit correctly. She came over, laid her palm flat against the granite for a long moment, and then tapped a single spot. "It wants to rest its heart there," she said. The apprentice shifted his lever by an inch, and the stone settled into place with a gentle sigh. She did not move the stone. She listened to it.


## 18. A Tapestry of Time [T2]

I have been here long enough now to understand how the island keeps itself hidden. It is not magic. It is logistics.

The fog comes first. A species of dragon the Manaïari call the Fog-Weavers produces it: a deep, rolling mist that rings the entire coast. From the outside, it looks like bad weather. From the inside, it is perfectly clear. I walked to the shore once and stood at the border. On my side, a sunny afternoon. On the other side, a storm that wasn't a storm. The division was sharp enough to put your hand through.

Above the fog, sentinels. Dragons with vision sharp enough to spot a ship at a distance I could not estimate. They watch the ocean the way the harbour master watches the fleet.

If a ship enters the fog, its crew falls asleep. I experienced this myself on the way here and can confirm it is not unpleasant. Nobody has explained the mechanism to my satisfaction, though I have my suspicions about certain small, drowsy-looking dragons I have seen nesting in the mist.

The Ship-Shifters are the largest living things I have seen. I have watched them from the cliffs, and even at a distance the scale is difficult to accept. A single one could wrap its claws around a merchant vessel the way a bird grips a twig. They lift the entire ship and fly it to the far side of the continent. They set it down in open water, pointed in roughly the direction it was already headed. Before the crew wakes, smaller dragons leave kindnesses: a shoal of fish herded into the ship's path, a tailwind arranged, freshwater tanks topped up by a convenient rain. Nothing a sailor would question. Good weather, good luck, good sea. Sailors love to take credit for all three.

During storm season, the Fog-Weavers work hardest. I watched three fly in tight formation through sheets of rain, weaving the fog thicker with every pass. Like someone knitting a wall in a hurricane.

And then there is the Elder. She is always the same dragon. Scales the colour of old jade, eyes that seem to be looking at something behind you. She approaches each sleeping human and blinks once, slowly. On very rare occasions, she nods. When she nods, that human is carried to a village and given a bed. I was profoundly asleep when it happened to me, but I remember the nod. A slight tilt of a vast head, a flicker in those ancient eyes. I have no idea what she saw in me. I rather wish I had been awake to ask, but I suppose that is the point. You do not get to audition.


## 19. The Journey [T1]

I hired a boat in Wellington. A small fishing vessel, a crew of three. I told them I was looking for an island that did not appear on any chart. The captain looked at me, looked at the compass, and named a price that suggested he thought I was mad but preferred to be paid for his trouble. Fair enough.

For the first four days the eye pulled south-west. You hold it up, look through it, and the world in one direction is ever so slightly brighter than the rest. More vivid. As if one slice of the horizon is more real than the others. You turn until the colours sing, and that is your heading. On the fifth morning the bright patch swung north. I adjusted the course. On the sixth it swung back. On the seventh it pointed, briefly and with apparent conviction, straight down. The captain stopped asking questions after that. He just followed whatever direction I gave him and smoked his pipe.

I know now that we were being misdirected. The island's defences do not begin at the fog. They begin much further out, in the currents, in the confusion, in the gentle suggestion that perhaps you have made a mistake and should turn around. The compass was fighting through it. Some days it won. Some days the island did.

On the eleventh day we hit the fog. Thick, white, absolute. The crew grew quiet, then drowsy. I remember the youngest one sitting down mid-sentence and closing his eyes. Then the captain. Then the other. I held the compass to my eye and tried to stay awake. Through the glass, the world ahead was blazing with colour. I followed it as long as I could.

I woke in a stone room. The compass was in my hand, closed.

It took me three days to find out what happened to the crew. When Manami finally understood what I was asking, she looked almost amused. The ship had been carried safely past the island and set down in open water. The crew would have woken refreshed, confused, and with no memory of the fog. They were fine.


## 20. Not All Dragons [T1]

I find myself staring south more and more these days, a rather pointless exercise. Manami has made it quite clear that the southern lands are not for me. It wasn't a command, of course, but a warning delivered with such solemnity that it felt heavier than any law. I noticed she was watching my reaction carefully, as if my response would determine what she told me next. Some dragons, she explained, do not share their northern cousins' curiosity for humankind.

It isn't malice, not really. As Manami puts it, they are purists. They see Aizomea as a perfect, self-willed garden, and we humans are an unwelcome weed. Our very presence, our desire to build and order things, offends their love for the island's untamed state.

The border itself is not a line on a map. The Manaïari call it the Tangle. It's a patchwork of defences: one stretch of forest buzzes with the quiet menace of giant, iridescent wasps; the next is filled with vines as thick as pythons that grasp at sound. There is, inexplicably, a mile-wide meadow of tiny yellow flowers that cause catastrophic hiccups. _(I got too close. Twenty minutes of my life I shall not get back.)_ It is a place woven from a hundred different kinds of 'No'.

The reason for this great rift is, like so many things here, a story. It's told more like a poem than a history, a tale from the time after the Manaïari had settled. When they first cleared a field for a farm, teaching wild grasses to grow in neat, straight lines, some dragons saw a clever partnership. They drew closer. But others saw only a cage for the grass, a prison for the land. And in silent, sorrowful disagreement, they left. As one leaves a room where the music has changed.

The southern coast is not protected the way the north is. The Fog-Weavers keep the mist thin there, just enough to discourage a passing ship, not enough to hide a continent. The southern dragons prefer it that way. They do not want to be hidden. They want to be left alone, and the difference is significant. The tales say a handful of humans have reached the southern shore over the centuries. Ambitious sailors, naive explorers, or the occasional fool with more courage than sense. None of them left. The tales do not say what happened to them. Nobody asks.

It isn't all grim warnings, though. Manami finished her lesson with a story I haven't stopped thinking about. A generation ago, a child's ball rolled into the Tangle, caught by a thorny vine. The mother, knowing the rules, left a bowl of fresh milk at the border. The next morning, the ball was sitting on the grass. The milk was gone, and the empty bowl had been filled with a few colourful, fallen leaves. A bargain, it seems, was struck.


## 21. The Knotted Cord [T3]

The children have been running about all week with fistfuls of what looks like captured clouds --- wisps of impossible colour spilling onto the paths. It is dragon-nest fuzz, shed by the young ones, and the women spin it into thread whilst murmuring songs that have no words.

They are preparing for the Knotted Cord.

A dragon's memory, it turns out, is a strange and selective thing. They can recall the shape of a continent shifting over millennia, but will forget what they ate for breakfast. The small, fleeting moments of a year slip through their grasp like fine sand. So the Manaïari have made themselves the keepers of the dragons' little memories. And in return, the dragons repay the favour with the stories of the earth itself.


We gathered at dusk. Or rather, I arrived at dusk. The others arrived when they arrived. No event on this island begins at its announced time. I have tested this rigorously. It is not rudeness. It is something closer to a philosophical position on the nature of time, which I find both admirable and infuriating.

A vast, open-air amphitheatre cupped between three mossy hills. Manami sat beside me without a word. A bonfire blazed in the centre. The dragons rested along the slopes, their immense forms like smaller hills themselves under the starlight.

Around the outer circle, stone archways housed the racks --- hundreds of them, holding thousands of cords, each representing a year, swaying in the evening breeze. An outdoor library of memory, open to the elements.

A teenage girl stood first. She spoke of practising a complex, sorrowful tune on her flute, a piece she was immensely proud of. A great Boulderback dragon, known for its granite stoicism, had been listening from a nearby ledge. When she finished her masterpiece, the dragon offered its review: a single, enormous, jaw-stretching yawn. She later saw the same dragon completely mesmerised by a toddler banging a spoon on a pot. The village roared. The knot-keeper, an old man with hands the size of dinner plates, tied a knot with a comical central hole. A knot for 'unimpeachable taste'.

The Boulderback watched from its hill, stony face impassive. But its eye, reflecting the bonfire, was not just watching. It was imprinting. The sound of the story, the shape of the knot --- all of it being filed away in that vast, slow mind.

A small boy jumped up next, to tell of losing a shiny button, only for a dragon to return it the next day in a glistening pile containing two shells, a bit of sea glass, and a very confused crab. A quick, tight knot was tied with a tiny pebble worked into it.

A fisherman stood next, quieter than the others. He told of a dragon, a long, silver-scaled thing, that had followed his boat every morning for an entire season. It never took the fish, never capsized the nets. It simply swam alongside, just below the surface, close enough to touch. Then one morning, it wasn't there. He never saw it again. He didn't know why it came, or why it left. The knot-keeper tied a long, loose loop with no bead, no pebble. A knot for something that has no ending.

Later, an old dragon shared a story. Through a Listener, who closed her eyes, placed a hand on the dragon's flank, and spoke what she felt. A story of an age, not a year: how the mountain to the west got its crooked peak. Rock and ice and slow, geological time. For this, the knot-keeper tied a simple, elegant loop of deep blue, signifying a piece of ancient truth. His enormous fingers moved like they were threading a needle.

They trade mayflies for mountains. They swap anecdotes for epochs. Humans offer the fleeting, precious spirit of their year, and in return, the dragons give them the soul of the world.


## 22. The Argument [T3]

Manami and I disagreed today. About what, I will not record. She is right. She is always right. This is the most infuriating thing about her.


## 23. Soaring with the Sky [T3]

**The Dragon's Sneeze**

I stood on a cliff today and very nearly forgot to breathe. Below, a young woman stood at the edge, clad in a harness of leather and shimmering blue metal ribs. She wasn't preparing to jump, not really. She was waiting.

A huge dragon lumbered up from the valley floor and launched into the sky with a downbeat of its wings that shook the rock I stood on. And in that precise moment, the woman simply... fell. For a terrifying second, she plunged towards the rocks. But then the vortex of air left by the dragon caught her, a dandelion seed in an invisible hand, and she was thrown upwards, laughing, into the sun. The dragon glanced back, and I could have sworn it winked.

**The Village Fling**

In the canyons on the other side of the spire, they don't ask the dragons or the earth for a lift. They ask the trees. The Manaïari have woven a monstrous slingshot between two ancient, rubbery trees. It takes a whole team of laughing men and women, chanting in rhythm, to pull the thing taut.

Today's flyer was a grandmother, strapped into the leather pouch with the calm dignity of someone boarding a train. An elder woman gave a final shout, the rope was cut, and with a deep, resonant *thrum* that vibrated in my chest, the grandmother was flung into the sky. Not with a scream, but with a delighted 'Wheee!' that echoed off the canyon walls.

**The Earth's Hiccup**

We travelled to the smoking plains, where the air smells of sulphur and creation. Here, flight is a far more serious, punctual affair. The Manaïari have built intricate metal platforms over geysers that erupt with hot air instead of water.

I watched a man in a sleek, insect-like glider stand perfectly still in a launch chamber. The ground began to tremble. A low rumble grew, and then with a deafening *WHOOSH*, a column of air shot him vertically into the sky. He went straight up, a rocket of bone and leather, for hundreds of feet before his machine tipped into a silent, effortless glide. Less a village fête, this one. A piece of clockwork powered by the island's own hiccup.

**A Conversation with the Wind**

Three launches, three entirely different methods. It strikes me now that the launch is unimportant. It is merely the opening line of a conversation.

The Manaïari do not fly *through* the air. They fly *with* it. I saw the grandmother from the canyon adjust the tips of her glider's wings --- just slightly --- to ask a thermal for a little more height. I saw the woman from the cliff lean into a crosswind, listening to its suggestion before offering her own.

Their gliders, with those intricate blue metal frames, seem to be extensions of their own senses. The dragons, I believe, did not teach the Manaïari physics. They taught them a language.

I asked Manami if she flies. She looked at me as if I'd asked whether she breathes.


## 24. The Amulet's Promise

Every Manaïari I have met wears the same hook of blue stone around their neck. It is not jewellery. It is an anchor and a compass. A promise made while touching the amulet, I am told, cannot be broken. It is a piece of the island's very heart, worn against their own.

They are not given out on a birthday, or at a specific age. A child receives their amulet when the elders---and, it seems, the dragons themselves---deem them ready. It is a rite of passage measured not in years, but in maturity and understanding. To receive one is to be fully seen, to belong not just to a family but to the land itself. To lose one, I am told, is unthinkable. Nobody would explain what happens. They just touched their own and changed the subject. I wonder if Nangula had one. I never saw her wear it, but then she was good at keeping things close.

## 25. A Trial of Fire and Ice

I was finally permitted to watch an amulet being made. They brought me to a clearing at dawn. Two dragons were already waiting.

The fire dragon was broad, dark, still. The only thing moving was one back leg, tapping the ground like a metronome. A raw blue stone, selected for its purity, was placed between them. The fire dragon exhaled. Controlled, focused heat. The stone began to glow. Just as it reached white-hot, the second dragon, whose scales shimmered with frost, breathed on it. The cold cracked the air itself. I felt it in my teeth from twenty feet away.

Most stones shatter. This one held. What was left was no longer fragile. A resilient, deep blue crystal, infused with the opposing natures of fire and ice. A carver took it from there, chiselling it into the familiar hook shape. Each one similar. Each one different.

## 26. The Humming Season

My own amulet---a gift of deep trust, placed around my neck by an elder woman while Manami stood watching with an expression I had never seen on her before---began to glow last night. A soft, internal light, as if it held a captured moonbeam. All across the village, other amulets did the same. This is the sign.

The dragons felt it too. One by one, they found a comfortable spot, curled their vast bodies into circles, and began to hum. It started as a low thrumming, barely a sound at all. The water in my cup trembled, then began to dance in tiny concentric rings. Soon, it swelled into a single, continuous chord that resonated through the very rock of the islands. High on the floating mountains, the caves where the stone is born pulsed with the same faint light. The Manaïari say this is the sound of the world being re-tuned, the great resonance that keeps their home afloat.

Their role in this is one of quiet guardianship. They move silently, ensuring absolute peace, leaving offerings of spiced honeyed milk for when the dragons awaken, several days later, thirsty from their work. It is a time of deep, collective meditation. Even the dogs sit still, which is how you know it's serious.

*(Editor's note: My grandmother's amulet was in the trunk. It still glows faintly each year around the same date, though I am nine thousand miles from Aizomea.)*

## 27. The Festival of Sniffles

But the season is not so serene for everyone. For a particularly fluffy species of dragon, the great resonance triggers a rather spectacular allergic reaction. For them, this is the Runny Nose Season.

I was sitting in the silence when the first sneeze came. It echoed off three hillsides. The dragon responsible, large and fluffy with watering eyes, looked wretched. A woman appeared within seconds carrying a steaming cauldron of spicy soup. The dragon drank. Sneezed again. Drank more. Whilst most Manaïari were tiptoeing in silence, a small crew rushed between the afflicted dragons with more cauldrons and sympathetic clucking.


## 28. Forest Dragon Dental Hygiene [T3]

Dental hygiene is, improbably, one of the great cultural achievements of Aizomea. You can tell it is morning in the forest district by the blue bubbles rising through the canopy.

The forest dragons brush their teeth every morning with a paste of chewed grass, fresh mint, and blue stone dust. They learnt the brushing from the Manaïari, who use a simple twig and take a minute or two about it. The blue stone was the dragons' own addition. They chew for twenty minutes. They have developed a technique involving three jaw rotations and a tongue sweep that I cannot replicate despite trying. Manami finds the whole thing mildly insulting. "We taught them," she says. "Now they act as if we do it wrong."

One dragon has appointed itself dental inspector. After brushing, it walks down the line and taps each dragon's teeth with a claw, peering into mouths with an air of absolute authority. It has no qualifications. It started doing this one day and not a single dragon had the nerve to refuse. Dragons twice its size sit with their jaws open, meek as lambs. One received a disapproving tap on a molar and slunk away to brush again.

I examined a dragon's teeth up close. Centuries old. Not a single cavity. Not a chip. Not a stain. I thought about my dentist in Harley Street. I thought about the state of British teeth in general. I was, for the first time since arriving here, genuinely cross. It is not just the teeth, either. Nobody here seems to ache, or limp, or cough through winter. The blue stone, it turns out, bonds to the enamel over years and hardens it until the oldest dragons have teeth that can bite through stone. And at night, sleeping dragons bare their teeth just slightly, and the blue stone glows. A faint perimeter of light rings the nesting site. Nothing crosses it. It is a fence made of smiles.

Naturally, I tried the paste. It works extraordinarily well. My teeth have never felt cleaner. The side effect is that I smiled blue for the rest of the day. Manami took one look at me and laughed so hard she had to sit down. I kept my mouth shut for the evening. I used the paste again the next morning. And the morning after that. Within a week I had joined the line: thirty dragons and one Englishwoman at the river's edge, all chewing in silence as the sun came up. Nobody objected to my presence. It is the most peaceful fifteen minutes of my day, and I intend to continue the practice for as long as I have teeth.

*(Editor's note: When Lady Pepper passed away in 1985, aged eighty-four, she had a full set of teeth that would have been the envy of a woman half her age.)*


## 29. Medicine and Healing [T3]

I broke my wrist in my second month. A stupid fall, a wet rock, an angle that made a sound I would rather not describe. In London, this would have meant six weeks in plaster and a great deal of complaining. Here, a woman arrived within minutes, felt the bone with fingers that could have cracked walnuts, and set it with a single firm movement that I felt in my teeth. Another bound it in a poultice of crushed leaves and blue stone dust. An ice dragon breathed on the swelling, a controlled cold so precise it barely stirred the hair on my arm. I was using the hand again in nine days. There are no doctors here. There are no appointments. The right people appear, and between them they know everything. I asked who organises this. Nobody organises it.

Dragons heal themselves, and not always in ways I can explain. One, suffering from something nobody could identify, climbed into a tree --- a creature the size of a barn, in a tree --- and hung upside down from a branch for an entire day. The branch held. The dragon recovered. Half of what the Manaïari know about healing, they learnt by following a sick dragon and doing whatever it did. Animals have been doing medicine longer than we have. They just never felt the need to write it down.

The thing I find hardest to write about is what they do for those who are unwell in spirit. I do not have a proper word for it. The Manaïari do not seem to distinguish between a broken bone and a broken heart. A person who is struggling is given company. Someone sits with them. Sometimes a Listener. Sometimes a friend. Sometimes a dragon, who lies nearby and breathes. Nobody tells them to cheer up. Nobody offers advice. They stay, for as long as staying is needed. I have seen this work on grief, on exhaustion, on the kind of deep, shapeless sadness that has no obvious cause and no obvious cure. I have never seen anyone here suffer alone.


## 30. The Collective Sigh [T3]

It happens without warning. On still, humid afternoons, every dragon in the valley exhales at the same moment. A long, low sigh that lasts perhaps three seconds. There is no wind. No movement. But for a moment I could have sworn my hair shifted, and the air tasted different. The dragons don't look at each other afterwards. They don't seem to have coordinated. They all felt the same thing at the same time. Not a soul can explain it. I asked a shaman. I asked the elders. I asked everyone I could find. They all shrugged.

For about ten minutes after, everything is slightly sharper. Colours a touch brighter. The cliff moss smells stronger. My tea tasted better, though it was the same cup I had been drinking before. Then it fades, and the world returns to normal, and you are left wondering whether the dragons just cleaned something you cannot see, or reset it.

Cassius, you would have measured it. I just sat there.

*(Editor's note: My grandfather Cassius died in 1954. She never stopped writing to him.)*



## 31. Dragons Outside Aizomea [T2]

Some dragons leave. This should not have surprised me, but it did. I had spent so long thinking of Aizomea as a closed world, a secret kept shut, that I forgot the obvious: the secret has legs. And wings. And, in some cases, flippers.

They go for all sorts of reasons, or for no reason at all. Some are curious. Some are restless. One, Manami told me, left because it didn't like the weather. The Fog-Weavers make no effort to stop them. There is a particular tilt in the way a dragon flies that signals intention, and the mist parts. No ceremony. No permission. Some stay away for years. Some for centuries. One, I am told, departed roughly five hundred years ago and came back smelling faintly of olive oil. It settled into its old sleeping spot as if it had been gone an afternoon.

The Manaïari believe dragons follow the blue stone's call across oceans, a frequency too low for human ears that carries like a pulse through the water. A lighthouse you hear rather than see. I think my compass works on the same principle. The eye that brought me here was not pointing north. It was looking.

I have spent years cataloguing dragons outside of Aizomea. Nangula spent decades before me. And before her, centuries of mythology: sea serpents in naval logs, gargoyles that turned out not to be stone, carved dragon prows on merchant ships. We knew they were real.
The whole bestiary I spent my career assembling was just the guest book of a place I hadn't found yet.

The hunting looks different from this side, too. I knew the numbers. I knew about the tail feathers for hats, the wings ground into paste, the wretched Hans the Hunter dynasty, whose sons and grandsons walked into the same jaws with the same idiotic confidence, generation after generation, as if stupidity were the one trait the family reliably passed down. But I had only ever seen the damage from the outside. Here I see the ones who came back. The scars are different when you recognise the animal. One returned carrying a musket ball in its shoulder, centuries old, grown over with scale like bark over a nail. It had simply kept flying. Somebody ought to do something about this. Somebody with more authority than a woman with a notebook and a loud opinion.

*(Editor's note: She did.)*


## 32. The Sea: The Coast [T1-T2]

The coast is where the secret ends. You can stand on the beach and see the wall of mist a few hundred yards out, a clean line between this world and the one I came from. I go there sometimes when I need to remember that the rest of the world exists. It helps less than it used to.

I am not always alone when I come. There is a young man I see sometimes, standing at the water's edge, looking out. Not at the sea. Past it. Manami says a few leave. Most come back.

The children here learn to swim before they can walk. This is not a saying. I watched a man place a baby in shallow water and it moved with a confidence that made me, a grown woman who learnt to swim at nine in a municipal pool in Surrey, feel personally inadequate. The sea is not feared here. It is the garden.

Fishing is done in partnership with the sea dragons. Not riding them. Working alongside them, the way a farmer works with a sheepdog. The dragons herd fish towards the boats, and the fishermen read their movements to know where to cast. I sat in a boat and watched the whole exchange. Not a word spoken, not a signal I could see. Just splashes, and adjustments, and a catch so large the boat sat low on the way home. There is a tradition that the first fish of the day is thrown back --- not to the sea, but to the nearest dragon. I asked a fisherman why. "Manners," he said. On the way back in, the crew sang. I am starting to think silence during work is considered rude here. The harvesters sing, the fishermen sing, the weavers sing. Even the mending women keep a tune going while they settle an argument. Only the Listeners are quiet, and that is their job.

The tides here do not follow the moon the way I expect. The Manaïari say the sea dragons affect the currents. Whether this is fact or folklore, I cannot tell. I have stopped trying to distinguish between the two.

There are dragons out there that never come ashore. I see them from the cliffs sometimes, vast shapes moving just below the surface, turning the water a shade darker as they pass. The legends say some of them are older than the island itself. At night, their calls carry up the cliff face. It sounds like whalesong, but deeper, slower. Not sadder. Just older.

Even the sand has a faint blue cast in certain lights. The shells too. The whole coastline looks like a painting someone mixed slightly too much blue into. And sometimes, after a storm, things wash up. Driftwood with barnacles from distant oceans. A length of rope. Once, a child's shoe. The Manaïari collect these with quiet fascination. Fragments of a world they know exists but have chosen not to join.


## 33. On Dragon Eggs [T3]

I have spent some time now studying dragon eggs, and the first thing to say is that there is no such thing as a dragon egg. There are hundreds of kinds, and they share almost nothing with each other.

Some are stone-hard. Some are rubbery. Some are soft and velvety, like the skin of a peach. Most are heavy, far heavier than they look, as if packed with everything the dragon will eventually become. I tried to lift one once and could not get it off the ground. A live egg is warm and has a faint pulse you can feel in your palms, slow and steady.

There is one kind that floats. The shell contains traces of blue stone, enough to make it lighter than air. In a cave, it drifts to the ceiling and stays there, bobbing gently, like a balloon at the end of a party. Untethered outdoors, it will rise steadily until it is a speck against the sky. The Manaïari keep them on strings, tied to a stone or a low branch, where they hover at head height, turning slowly in whatever breeze passes through. I saw a row of them outside a house once, tethered to a railing, bumping softly against each other. The woman who lived there said two of them had been floating there for years. The third, she added, had started to kick.

Most eggs never hatch, though. This is not a sad thing, or at least the Manaïari do not treat it as one. An unfertilised egg hardens over the weeks, turning to solid stone, cool and silent. These petrified eggs are everywhere. On shelves. In gardens. Propping open doors. They are smooth, often beautiful, and entirely ordinary.

Except when they are not. Every family seems to have a story about an egg assumed to be stone that one day, without warning, hatched. A man told me he came home to find his shelf ornament had grown legs and was eating his curtains. Manami's neighbour kept one as a doorstop for eleven years. It is now the size of a large dog and still sleeps in the doorway, which may be force of habit.

A village might see one or two hatchings a year. Each one draws the whole community, because you never quite know when the next will come, or from where. Even, it seems, from the furniture.


## 34. The Grand Unveiling [T1]

Today I witnessed something few in the world have (I seem to be saying that often lately, but this one feels different): I saw a dragon hatch.

It was also one of the funniest scenes. In a stone courtyard hemmed in by house walls on three sides, children were playing a game I'd describe as a mix of rugby and soccer, with the added twist of trying to get as many bounces off the walls as possible. It took me a few minutes to realise the 'ball' was, in fact, an egg. A dragon egg.

Only, this was no fragile bird's egg, nor one of the stone-hard varieties. It was bouncy. Like rubber. As the game picked up, the egg seemed to take on a life of its own, adding a hilarious randomness to proceedings. After one epic kick, it bounced straight back into the kicker. As if hitting back.

That's when an elder man, who had been watching with an amused yet attentive eye, interrupted the game. The egg lay still on the ground. The children, a few other villagers, and even a couple of dragons gathered around. The egg, quite round until now, began to change shape from the inside. A very sharp claw pierced through and tore a larger hole.

From it, an already chunky (though small) dragon walked out. Or perhaps, stumbled its way out, fumbling, rolling, as if it were still dizzy from the bouncing. Everyone burst into laughter. River, you would have loved this. Cendre, you would have tried to take it home. I must bring you here one day. Though I supposed that requires being able to come back. Or leaving, for that matter.

Talking more about dragons' births with the Manaïari, I realise it's as much about folklore and community as it is biology. After that chaotic moment, Manami and I sat for hours over what passes for tea here --- a pale gold infusion of sun-dried cliff moss that tastes faintly of honey and smoke --- as she shared other hatching stories. I filled six pages of notes. My zoology professors would have wept, or resigned.

There is one species, she said, that is 'grown' in a shared garden. The eggs are velvety pods, like large melons, and the whole village tends to them. Apparently, a great deal of singing is involved. One morning, a pod will 'bloom' into a light pink, silken flower, and a dragon lies sleeping at its heart. (The 'flower', Manami noted, is actually the tip of its tail).

For another, the egg simply contains... another egg. Which itself contains another. Each nested shell is smaller and more ornate than the last. While the first egg might look like a rock as large as our dining table, the dragon that emerges from the innermost one is no bigger than my thumb.

Then, there is the Stone-egg, so hard it must be sung open. When it's time, the mother starts with a low note, and the whole village joins in, tapping in rhythm until you can feel the ground vibrate. Only then does the egg unfold, petal by stone petal. Here in Aizomea, a dragon, it seems, is born into a chorus. I filled six pages of notes. My zoology professors would have wept.


## 35. Hatchlings and Milk [T1]

There is a young dragon in the village that is, as far as I can tell, made entirely of appetite. It hatched three weeks before I arrived, and it has not stopped eating since.

I watched it work through an entire pen of goats this morning. It latched onto the first with a grip that made the goat's eyes go very wide, emptied it in minutes, released it, and moved to the next. Like a milkman working through the bottles on a doorstep, except in reverse. The goat stood there, deflated, looking personally offended. Nobody seemed alarmed. A man sighed, picked the dragon up (it is still small enough to carry, though not for much longer), and set it on the grass. It burped. The goat nearest to it took a step back.

I can already hear some future colleague insisting that dragons, being non-mammalian, cannot possibly drink milk. To which I say: come here, watch a hatchling drain a goat in four minutes flat, and then explain to the hatchling that it is doing something biologically impossible. I am sure it will be fascinated.

There is also a tree here whose sap is thick, white, and sweet, and which the Manaïari call dragon milk. The hatchlings are mad for it. I was given a gourd of the stuff and told to try feeding one myself. I sat on the ground and held the gourd out. The dragon approached with the caution of something that suspects a trap but is too hungry to care. It drank the entire gourd in one long, unbroken gulp, then sat back and looked at me. Not with gratitude, exactly. With assessment. Then it walked off. I assumed that was the end of it.

Ten minutes later, it came back carrying a small purple stone in its mouth, which it dropped in my lap and left again. I looked at the stone. I looked at the dragon. I looked at the woman who had been watching.

She shrugged. "It likes you."

I have kept the stone.

*(Editor's note: The stone was in the trunk when I found it.)*


## 36. Egg Kintsugi [T3]

Manami's house has four of them. Repaired dragon eggshells, displayed on a high shelf, each one glowing faintly blue along its cracks. I mistook them for pottery the first time. They are not pottery. They are birth records.

After a hatching, every fragment of shell is gathered and reassembled with a paste of crushed blue stone and tree resin, applied with a fine brush, filling every crack and seam. When it dries, the shell is whole again, but the breaks remain visible, glowing. You can read the birth in the pattern. A violent kick leaves jagged lines. A slow emergence leaves long, graceful curves. The bouncy egg I saw the children playing with left a web of tiny fractures so fine it looked like lace.

Given how rarely dragons are born, a house with four eggs on its shelf might represent centuries of family. The oldest repaired egg I have seen sits in the weaving hall, near the tapestries. The shell has yellowed. The blue stone lines have deepened to almost black. Nobody touches it. It is not fragile. It is simply that old things deserve stillness.

I watched a woman work on one for an entire afternoon. Her hands were steady, her brush strokes precise, her concentration total. Hana, Manami's daughter, now a young woman herself, sat beside her learning the strokes. There are perhaps three people in the village trusted with the task. It is not a skill one picks up. It is a skill one earns.

It reminds me of kintsugi. I wonder which came first.


## 37. The Awkward Teenager [T3]

Mes petits monstres, you will appreciate this one.

I have written at length about hatchlings, and it is easy to do so, because hatchlings are ridiculous and perfect and one cannot help but love them. Nobody warns you about what comes next.

Adolescent dragons grow in bursts, and the bursts are not always symmetrical. One wing will outpace the other for weeks. A tail doubles in length overnight whilst its owner is still turning corners at yesterday's radius. They crash into things. They knock over market stalls. The Manaïari have a specific word for the sound of a teenage dragon misjudging a doorway. I heard it four times before lunch.

They also sulk. With commitment. A teenage dragon in a mood will drape itself over the nearest structure and go boneless. Rooftops. Bridges. One had claimed an entire hillside, chin on the ground, eyes half-closed, radiating the kind of theatrical despair that would make a Romantic poet look restrained. Its mother tried nudging it. It went limp. Like trying to move a wet carpet, except the carpet weighs several tonnes and is doing it on purpose.


## 38. The Bonfire Courtship [T3]

Courtship among the fire-breathing species is, essentially, arson.

The male spends days assembling a structure from whatever burns well. Dry grass, shed bark, stolen rope, the odd fence post. Aesthetic quality is irrelevant. What matters is combustibility. He lays this offering at the feet of the female. She inspects it. If she approves, she incinerates it with a single, concentrated breath. The bigger the fire, the better. If she walks away, he must dismantle the whole thing and start over. I watched one male build and rebuild four times in a single week. By the end he looked less like a suitor and more like a man who has been asked to repaint the kitchen. The ice dragons, meanwhile, spend the entire courtship season on fire duty, dousing the inevitable bush fires with expressions of weary contempt.

The best story, though, belongs to a male who built something so elaborately flammable that it caught fire from the afternoon sun before the female had even arrived. He was sitting beside a pile of ashes when she finally came down from the ridge. She looked at the ashes. She looked at him. She stayed. Apparently, the intention counts.


## 39. The Fire Brigade [T3]

Nobody founded the fire brigade. Nobody appointed anyone. The ice dragons started doing it because someone had to, and they were the only ones who could, and they have never forgiven the fire-breathers for making it necessary.

During courtship season, they patrol in pairs. Some walk slow, deliberate circuits of the village. Others fly wide loops over the valleys, scanning for smoke. Their technique is a short, controlled blast of frost aimed at the base of the fire. Efficient. Precise. Joyless. They extinguish bush fires the way a librarian shushes a talker.

One courtship offering was built too close to a storage hut. The female torched it, as is tradition. The hut nearly went with it. An ice dragon arrived at full sprint, the fastest I have ever seen one move, and froze the entire offering, the hut, and a passing goat in a single breath. The goat was fine. Cold, but fine. The courtship was deemed successful anyway.


## 40. The Old Souls [T3]

Old dragons, I have decided, are not wise. They are simply old, and have had more time to become themselves.

The oldest dragon in the village is also the most shameless cheat at every game the Manaïari play. Board games, dice, children's guessing games. She wins them all by methods nobody can quite prove are dishonest. She has been alive for centuries. She has had time to perfect the art. The children adore her. The adults are suspicious. River would be on the dragon's side. She always takes the side of the one having the most fun. This is not wisdom. This is cunning with a very long head start.

There is one who has spent fifty years pretending to be a boulder. Travellers sit on it to rest. It waits, sometimes for hours, then shifts. Just slightly. Just enough. The scream, apparently, never gets old. The Manaïari put a sign up. It ate the sign.

Another parks itself at the dock each morning and watches the boats come in. If the catch is good, it does nothing. If the catch is poor, it exhales slowly through its nostrils, a long, whistling sigh that can be heard across the harbour. The fishermen pretend not to notice. It has never caught a fish in its life. It has never been wet. It contributes nothing to the village except disapproval, and the village adores it for this.

And then there is the one in the square. White-scaled, motionless, so old that children climb on it and cats sleep between its claws. Someone had hung a garland of flowers over one horn. It is easy to mistake it for a monument. But every night, when the village goes dark, it opens one eye. It scans the houses, slowly, one by one. Then it closes the eye and sleeps again. The Manaïari call it the village's second heartbeat. I take back what I said. That one might be wise after all.


## 41. Dragon Caves [T3]

Each cave is shaped by its occupant the way a chair is shaped by the person who sits in it for forty years. The walls are worn smooth where a flank has pressed against them nightly. The floor is scooped where a tail curls. You can walk into an empty cave and know, from the grooves and the faint smell, what lived there and roughly how long ago it left.

There are, broadly, three kinds. High caves in the mountain faces, open to the wind, favoured by the flying species. Forest caves at ground level, damp and overgrown, where the entrance is sometimes hidden by vines and you don't realise you've walked into someone's home until someone clears their throat. And deep caves, far underground, where the blue stone in the walls glows brighter the further you descend. I visited one that was lit entirely by the stone. No sunlight. No fire. Just blue, everywhere, as if I had walked inside a sapphire.

The walls are marked. Claw marks, cut deep into the rock, in patterns that repeat across different caves. I have spent weeks sketching them. Some are long, deliberate grooves, scored with obvious care. Others are clusters of short scratches, grouped in arrangements too regular to be accidental. In one cave I found what I can only describe as a mural --- an entire wall covered in overlapping marks of different depths and ages, the oldest nearly worn flat, the newest still sharp enough to catch the light. It looked like generations of dragons had been adding to the same surface, each one leaving its mark over or beside the last. Whether this is art, or territory, or record-keeping, or all three, I cannot say. Manami thinks they are scratching posts. I think Manami is being modest about what the dragons are capable of.

In the communal caves, where dozens sleep together, there is an etiquette I am still learning. Each dragon has its corner. The boundaries are invisible but absolute. Noise is tolerated up to a point. Snoring, apparently, is not grounds for eviction, but it is grounds for being nudged, repeatedly, until you roll over.

My favourite thing about the caves is the entrance. Dragons sit at the mouth of their caves in the evenings the way old women sit outside their houses in a village in the south of France. Watching. Commenting silently on passers-by. Occasionally rumbling at one another. They have favourite spots. They have routines. One dragon has sat in the same position at the same cave mouth every evening for, I am told, longer than anyone can remember. It is not guarding anything.


## 42. Nests of Meaning [T3]

The legends are---as usual---entirely wrong: Dragons do not hoard gold.

They very much hoard, but instead of cold, lifeless metal, they collect what can only be described as piles of worthless knick-knackery. Trinkets. Bric-a-brac, as my mother would say. Not a single piece of gold among any of them (despite the stuff being everywhere else on this island).
They hoard memories.

From a young age, they keep their "treasure" in a seemingly random pile in their cave, yet they know exactly what is where, down to the last pebble. To them, it is the most precious thing in the world: their life story, their identity. You could tell everything about a dragon's personality just by 'reading' its nest. Was it a traveller? a parent? a romantic? a prankster?

In one pile, I noticed a set of false teeth. Gilt wire and porcelain. Its owner knows nothing of dentistry; it just enjoys the clack-clack-clack they make when nudged with a snout.

Another has a single petrified cuckoo's egg. It is convinced this is the pinnacle of avian comedy: a "joke egg" laid by a bird with a magnificent sense of humour.

There is this dragon that collects small black pebbles from the nearby riverbed, adding one every year. A dragon-scale, very slow hourglass.

The most inconvenient must be the entire iron rudder from a small ship. It's enormous. It is, I am quite certain, the dragon equivalent of a child's favourite stuffed bear. The dragon sleeps with one claw hooked fast through the tiller hole, terrified of floating away in its sleep.


## 43. How Dragons Die [T1]

I have been asking how dragons die. It is not a question the Manaïari enjoy, though not because it is sad. It is because the answer is not one answer.

Some fade. Over weeks, they grow quieter. They eat less. Their colour pales. The Listeners say the dragon's presence thins, until one morning the flank is cool. It is not sudden. It is not violent. It is a long exhale that, at some point, does not draw back in.

Some go to the sea. The dying dragon walks to the coast, enters the water, and swims out past the reef. The marine dragons rise to meet it. What happens beyond the fog line, nobody has seen. The fishermen say the water glows for a night afterwards, a deep blue, and then it stops.

Some choose. An old dragon will fly to a floating island, alone, without announcement, and not come back. The Manaïari do not follow. They watch from below until the shape disappears into the sky. There is no ceremony. There is no search. If the dragon wanted company, it would not have gone alone.

Rarest of all, a few turn to stone. It is slow, spanning decades. The tail stiffens first. Then the hind legs. Then the rest, creeping forward over years. The dragon lives through it, moving less and less, until one day it is still. What remains looks like a rock formation, a ridge in the shape of a sleeping animal. I have walked past two without realising what they were. Manami pointed them out. Once you see it, you cannot unsee it. The hills here have more faces than I thought.

And then there are the ones who, the Manaïari say, do not die at all. They become part of the island. The stone. The hum. The blue in the mountains. I asked Manami if she believed this. She looked at me as if I had asked whether she believed in the ground she was standing on. Nangula died three years ago, in a bed in Namibia, far from here. But I think part of her is in these mountains. I think she would have wanted that.


## 44. The Final Flight [T5]

I asked Manami what happens afterwards. She took me to a garden.

It looked like a nest that someone had forgotten, tucked into a shallow cave. But moss had crept in between the pebbles and trinkets, and tiny white flowers, and a vine that had wound itself around a piece of driftwood as if holding it upright. The whole thing was becoming something else, slowly, without anyone's permission.

The Manaïari tend these nest-gardens. Not with grief. They weed them. They water the moss. They bring their children to play among the objects, to pick up the stones and put them back, to ask what this or that thing meant. "So the memories don't get lonely," Manami said. She was pulling a weed as she said it, the way one does when saying something important and preferring not to make a fuss.


## 45. The Mouelleubleus [T3]

There is blue dust on my notebook again. A young dragon rubbed against my bag this morning, and now everything in it has a faint blue cast. My pencils. My spare shirt. The sandwich I was saving for later.

The Manaïari call them the Mouelleubleus. I asked Manami to translate. She said it means, roughly, "the soft blue ones," then paused and admitted it also sounds like the noise a cat makes when it is unimpressed. She suspects a child coined it. I suspect she is right.

They are dragons who have lived so long near blue stone that the pigment has seeped into their scales. The deeper the blue, the older the dragon, the longer it has slept and eaten and breathed in the stone's company. Among them, colour is biography. A pale blue is young, or a newcomer. A deep, almost black blue has been here since before anyone can remember. The young ones, naturally, cheat. They roll in blue dust to look older and more distinguished. It fools absolutely no one. The dust sits on top of the scales instead of inside them, and rubs off on everything. It is the dragon equivalent of a false moustache, and about as convincing.

## 46. The Dream Weaver [T3]

I woke with a sore back, a mild confusion about whether I had legs, and the faint, irrational certainty that I had been useful. The confusion wore off by evening. The certainty has not.

I had been dream-fishing. The children have a game: you find a sleeping dragon, lie down beside it, close your eyes, and wait. Most of the time, nothing happens. You just have a nap, which is its own reward. But once in a while, something catches. It doesn't work with every dragon, or every person, or every afternoon. Manami says some people are better at it than others, and children are better than adults, and tired people are better than alert ones, which may be why it tends to happen after lunch. The catch, when it comes, is not a story. It is a sensation. You don't dream about the dragon. You dream as the dragon. And whatever the dragon is dreaming, you become.

A boy told me he once dreamt he was a mountain for six hours. Not standing on a mountain. Being one. He felt rain on his northern face and sun on his southern one. He woke up stiff and convinced his left arm was older than his right. It took him two days to stop walking as if he weighed several thousand tonnes. A girl dreamt she was underwater, breathing easily, watching light bend above her. She spent the rest of the day walking slowly, as if still carrying the weight of a current, and refused to eat anything but fish for a week.

I tried it myself, beside a large, deeply asleep dragon in the afternoon sun. Nothing happened for a long time. Then I dreamt I was flying, which sounds marvellous until I tell you that the flying lasted approximately four seconds before the dragon's dream shifted and I spent the remaining hour as a warm rock being sat on by something enormous.

## 47. Walking Worlds [T3]

I spent an entire day following one. It moved so slowly I had to remind myself it was moving at all, a vast, low creature whose back had become a meadow. Grasses grew along its spine. Tiny white flowers I couldn't identify clustered between the shoulder blades. A sapling, no taller than my forearm, leaned slightly with each step, correcting itself as the dragon shifted weight, the way a sailor adjusts to a swell. Butterflies orbited it. I counted seventeen species of plant in an hour and stopped counting only because I ran out of page.

The relationship, I have come to understand, is not accidental. The algae that coat the lower scales clean them, slowly digesting the grime and parasites that would otherwise accumulate. The mushrooms that sprout along the flanks do something similar, breaking down dead scale and returning nutrients to the living surface beneath. The tiny insects pollinate the flowers, and the flowers produce a nectar that pools in the grooves between scales. Everything feeds everything else. It is biology so circular and so quiet that you could watch it for a week and still not be sure who is doing whom the favour.

At night, everything changes. I went back after dark and found the dragon asleep, perfectly still, and its back had become something else entirely. The algae glowed. A pale blue-green light traced every scale. The mushrooms gave off their own faint luminescence, a warmer tone, and between them tiny insects darted and paused and darted again, each one carrying its own small spark. There was an entire nocturnal world happening on the back of a sleeping animal, a country the size of a dining table, running on its own clock, utterly indifferent to mine.

I should also mention the tenant. A very small dragon, no bigger than a cat, lives permanently on this creature's back. It has hollowed out a nest between two mushroom clusters and, as far as I can tell, never touches the ground. I asked a man nearby if they were related. "No," he said. "It just moved in one day. The big one didn't seem to mind." I watched the small one emerge at dusk, stretch, eat something off a mushroom cap, and disappear back into its nest. It has the air of someone who has found the perfect flat and has no intention of ever leaving.


## 48. Field Note [T3]

Three dragons sleeping in a pile today. I counted the legs. There were too many.


## 49. The Skywings' Ballet [T3]

I watched a young dragon try to join a formation today. It was half a beat behind on every turn, banking too late, climbing too steeply. The others did not stop. They did not slow down. They adjusted around it, the way a river flows around a stone, and kept going. By the third pass, the young one had found the rhythm. By the fifth, I could no longer tell which one it was. I have not seen anything taught so gently. No correction. No instruction. Just space, made at exactly the right moment.

Some species perform at night. I sat on a ridge after dark and watched them cross the sky, their scales catching the moonlight and leaving trails behind them. Silver arcs, fading slowly, like letters in a language I almost recognised. I sat there long after they had gone, watching the last trails dissolve.


## 50. The Air Dancers [T3]

The females of this species write in the sky. Not metaphorically. They actually write --- great swooping sentences traced in flight, visible only in the way the light catches the faint shimmer they leave behind. A banking turn repeated three times means something different from one repeated twice. A sudden climb followed by a stall has a meaning I have not yet decoded. A long, unhurried glide means something close to contentment, or perhaps "the fish are good today." I'm still working out the grammar.

I spent a whole morning on a hilltop with my sketchbook, mapping their flight paths onto paper. Laid flat, the lines looked remarkably like calligraphy --- elegant, rhythmic, full of intention. I was rather pleased with myself. I'd filled four pages.

I showed them to Manami that evening. She studied the first page for a long moment, turned it upside down, and handed it back.

"That one says the hunting is good to the east."

I had been reading them backwards. All of them. Four pages of perfectly transcribed nonsense. Or rather, four pages of perfect sense, held the wrong way up. Which I suppose is a fair summary of my early years here.


## 51. The Lantern Keepers [T3]

On foggy nights, certain dragons glow. A warm amber light seeps through their belly scales, soft enough to read by, steady enough to follow home. They hover above the island paths, perfectly still, and if you happen to look up and catch one's eye, it drifts a little higher, as if you weren't supposed to notice. The Manaïari call them Lantern Keepers, though the dragons themselves seem to find the role beneath comment.

I climbed a hill one evening to see them from above. It had been raining, and the fog was thick enough to lose your hand in. But from the hilltop, the Lantern Keepers traced every path on the island in floating amber dots. The village below had disappeared entirely. Only the lights remained, suspended in the grey, a constellation that someone had dropped on the ground and forgotten to pick up. I sat there a long time. It is one of the quieter beautiful things I have seen here.

A child told me later, with great confidence, that the Lantern Keepers are not guiding anyone. They are afraid of the dark. They hover above paths because it means someone will always be walking beneath them, and they glow because they are nervous. I wrote this down. I think about it more often than I should.


## 52. Notes from the Interior: Day One [T3]

Manami has been promising to take me beyond the valley for weeks. Today she finally declared me ready, though she would not say what for. We argued about notebooks (she said two was plenty, I brought five) and left before the sun was fully up.

The continent, she tells me, takes roughly twenty days to cross on foot. Fifteen north to south. I did the arithmetic. If the pace is anything like ours, that makes it perhaps a hundred and fifty miles across. Larger than anything I was prepared for. I have been living in a corner and thinking it was the whole.

*(Editor's note: Having walked a fair portion of it myself, I believe this is generous. The landmass is closer to a hundred miles across. The southern half remains unmapped.)*

By midday we had reached a settlement tucked into a fold of hillside, smaller than ours. We stopped to refill our water and I nearly set down my bag on what I thought was a scorched log. It was not a log. It was a young dragon, and it was having a bad day.

It hiccupped. A jet of flame shot sideways and caught my bag strap. Manami put it out with her foot, casually, the way one stamps out a cigarette. The dragon stared at me with the round, horrified eyes of someone who has just sneezed on a stranger. It hiccupped again and set fire to a fern. A woman emerged from the nearest house carrying a bucket of sand, tossed it on the fern without breaking stride, and went back inside.

It cannot help it, they told me. It is young. The hiccups are involuntary, triggered by nothing in particular. Eating. Breathing. Existing. The settlement has adapted --- fireproof thatch, wide clearings, sand buckets on every corner. The dragon has positioned itself at the edge, as far from the houses as it can manage while still being part of things. It wants to belong. It just keeps accidentally setting the belonging on fire.


## 53. Notes from the Interior: Day Four [T3]

My knees would like it noted, for the record, that they did not agree to this.

The cliff village is carved vertically into a limestone face. Terraces, rooms, stairways, all going up. Dragons perch on the wide ledges at every level, tails hanging down like bunting. The only way up, if you are not a dragon, is a series of handholds worn smooth by centuries of use. Manami climbed it the way one climbs a staircase. I climbed it the way one climbs a cliff. Because it is one.

The view from the top is worth the legs. The forest we walked through stretches in every direction, dense and dark and old, the kind of forest that has never been cut and does not intend to be. Along the ridge to the east, a row of boulders sits in a line that is too even to be natural. I know what they are now.

On the way up I passed a dragon on a ledge that I would, in any other context, have described as terrifying. Enormous jaw. Heavy brow. The physique of something designed to be feared. ## 54. Notes from the Interior: Day Seven [T3]

We slept in the open last night. No village. No walls.

It was not the same as night at "home". No voices carrying. No firelight. Just the dark, and the things in it. I could hear breathing from several directions at once. None of it human. Something moved through the trees behind us around midnight, close enough to shift the branches overhead. We did not look. We did not speak. We lay still until it passed.

Dawn fixed everything. The river was gold with first light, and a dragon was standing in the shallows, drinking. It raised its head, looked at me, and breathed out a bubble. Then another. The whole bank smelled like my mother's garden in July. I sat downwind and sketched until Manami came to collect me.

In a clearing nearby, I found a ring of stones. Seven of them, each a different colour, arranged in a circle too precise to be accidental. They were heavy. Not something human hands could have placed.

## 55. Notes from the Interior: Day Eleven [T3]

Everything here is wet. The floors are wet. My notebooks are wet. I am wet. The lagoon village is built on stilts over a shallow bay, and the entire structure moves with the tide. Through the gaps in the floorboards I can see water, and through the water I can see dragons. I have been gripping the railing since I arrived. The children run between platforms barefoot and laughing. I hate them, gently.

Manami says there is a village further north that moves entirely. Follows a herd of grazing dragons, packs up every few months, relocates. I asked if we could visit. She said "next time," which in Manami's vocabulary sits somewhere between "perhaps" and "absolutely not."

The smallest dragon I have ever seen lives here. It is the size of a cat. It rules the dock. It struts along the railing, claims every post it passes, and produces what it clearly believes is a roar. The sound that comes out would not frighten a moth. The large dragons step around it with exaggerated care, which I first interpreted as respect. Having watched more closely, I now believe it is the same instinct that makes a person step around a very small, very angry dog. Nobody wants to be the one to break the spell.

There is one smaller still. I almost missed it. Eight centimetres, perched between the shoulder blades of a dragon the size of a cart horse. It appeared to be giving directions. The larger dragon appeared to be following them. I watched for ten minutes to be certain I was not hallucinating. I was not. The small one tapped the left shoulder. The large one turned left. I have no explanation for the power dynamics at work here, and I am not sure I want one.


## 56. Notes from the Interior: The Return [T3]

Sixteen days. Twelve villages. Four notebooks full. My feet ache in places I did not know feet could ache.

We covered, Manami estimates, perhaps a fifth of the northern half. A fifth. And every valley held something I had not seen in the one before. Different dragons, different shapes, different sizes, different habits. I have been cataloguing variations the way one catalogues stars. There are always more. And the south, the whole forbidden south, remains untouched, unseen, full of whatever it is that does not want to be known. I think about it more than I should.

There is a dragon in our village I have walked past a hundred times without paying attention. It sits in different spots throughout the day, head tilted, still. I used to think it was dozing. After the journey, I watched it properly. It is not dozing. It is listening. It moves every hour, settling into a new position, adjusting the tilt of its head like someone finding the sweet spot in a concert hall.

I followed it to its cave. Inside: nothing. No nest. No objects. No pebbles, no trinkets, no stolen cooking pots. Bare stone walls, worn smooth. I stood in the centre and clapped once. The sound came back to me three times, each echo cleaner than the last. Perfect acoustics. That is what it has been collecting. Not objects. Sound. The cave is its instrument, and it has spent years, maybe decades, finding the right one.

The best collection of nothing I have ever heard.

## 58. Dragon Body Language [T1-T2]

I have had to unlearn most of what I thought I knew about reading animals.

The first time a dragon bared its teeth at me, I backed into a wall. It took a full week before someone explained that baring teeth is how they say hello. By then I had greeted approximately forty dragons by retreating in terror. I have been making up for it since.

The tail is the most reliable indicator. Flat on the ground means contentment. Curled upward means alert. Tapping means bored, and if you have read my notes on boredom, you will know to take this seriously. Tail wrapped around another dragon means something close to "this one is mine and I am not discussing it."

Before sitting down, some dragons walk in a tight circle a few times, you know, like a dog would. This means something important is about to happen. The difficulty is that "important" covers everything from laying an egg to choosing a spot to nap. I once watched a dragon circle five times, lower itself with great ceremony, and fall asleep. I waited two hours for the important thing. It was the nap. The nap was the important thing.

Standing on one leg means "I disagree but I have decided not to argue." I saw an entire row of dragons standing on one leg during a village meeting once. I asked what was happening. "Democracy," Manami said.

I also saw two dragons press their foreheads together one morning, briefly, then walk away in opposite directions without looking back.


## 59. When a Dragon is Bored [T2-T5]

My darlings, if you ever wonder where you get it from, read this.

I have, after considerable fieldwork, compiled an unofficial guide to the stages of dragon boredom. I offer it here as a public service.

Stage one is the tail. A bored dragon taps its tail against the ground in a slow, irregular rhythm, the way a person drums their fingers on a table when the conversation has gone on too long. This is harmless. Stage two is rearrangement. The dragon begins moving nearby objects into patterns. Sticks. Pebbles. Someone's sandals. The patterns have no meaning. That is the point. Stage three is the stare. The dragon fixes its gaze on a specific building, or person, or goat, and does not look away. This can last hours. Stage four is the grin. If you see the grin, I am told, it is already too late.

I did not believe it until the laundry incident. A dragon, left unattended for a single afternoon, removed every piece of drying laundry in the village and arranged it into a single enormous nest on the roof of the meeting hall. Shirts, wraps, bedsheets, all sorted by colour. It sat in the middle looking tremendously pleased with itself. Getting the laundry back required negotiation. The dragon wanted three fish and a compliment. It got both.

A week later, another dragon spent a morning arranging fish bones, driftwood, and stolen cutlery into an elaborate structure on the village green. The Manaïari could not decide whether it was vandalism or art. They argued about it for days. The dragon, bored again by then, knocked the whole thing over and built a new one. It has been doing this for months. The village now has an involuntary rotating art gallery, and opinions remain divided.

The worst case I witnessed involved a dragon and a child, both bored, who found each other on a Tuesday morning. By noon they had redirected a stream into someone's kitchen. By mid-afternoon they had built a dam out of cooking pots. By evening they had taught a goat to climb onto a roof. The village held an emergency meeting. The dragon attended, looking innocent. The child did not attend. She was on the roof, with the goat.

The Manaïari solution to all of this is simple: give the dragon a job. Any job. One formerly destructive dragon is now the village bell. It bongs its own head against a rock at appointed hours. It takes this duty with tremendous seriousness, and has not rearranged anyone's laundry since. It has, however, developed a slight headache and what I can only describe as a sense of purpose.


## 60. The Mimicking Mouths [T5]

Three weeks ago, I hummed a hymn within earshot of a small dragon sitting on a wall. I thought nothing of it. The dragon has not stopped reproducing the tune since. It follows me. It perches outside my window at dawn and delivers it with a warbling vibrato that sounds like the organ at St. Mary's after the flood. I have tried ignoring it. I have tried shooing it. Last Tuesday, in desperation, I hummed a French lullaby, hoping to overwrite the hymn. It learned that one too, and now performs both, back to back, as a medley. It has also taught the hymn to two others. I have a small, atonal choir that accompanies me to breakfast. The children find this hilarious. I am running out of songs I am willing to sacrifice.


## 61. Bartering for Baubles [T4]

I am holding a pebble. It is perfectly round, grey, and according to Manami, rather valuable. She raised both eyebrows when she saw it, which is more alarm than I have ever seen her show. Something to do with its weight, or its colour, or possibly the roundness. She wasn't entirely clear, and I suspect the economy here resists explanation on purpose.

I got it from a dragon.

There is a stall at the market run by a dragon. I took Cendre and River there this morning. I use "run" loosely. It is a flat rock with objects arranged on it in what I can only assume is a system, though I couldn't tell you what kind. The dragon sits behind it with the air of a Bond Street jeweller. I offered a brass button from my coat. It studied the button, turned it over twice with one claw, and placed the pebble in front of me. A fair trade, apparently. Encouraged, I offered a second button. I was refused with a snort so forceful it blew my hair back. One button, one pebble. The window had closed.

I have, through no skill of my own, become a dragon of means. The button, meanwhile, has already been placed at the centre of the stall's display. Prime position. I walked past this morning and the dragon gave me a nod. Collegial. One trader to another. River had wandered off by then. I found her at the docks, talking to a young fisherman. They were laughing about something. She was trying out her three words of Manaïari. He was teaching her a fourth.

## 62. The Grand Gourmands [T4]

Dragons love ice cream. This is not a metaphor or an anthropomorphism. They love it the way I love a good book: without reservation, without judgement, and to the exclusion of all other priorities.

The island's signature flavour is blue stone. The dust is ground to a fine powder, stirred into a base of coconut milk and honey, and flash-frozen by an ice dragon whose involvement in the process can only be described as supervisory. It tastes of almost nothing at first. Then something cold and mineral blooms at the back of your throat, like licking a clean river stone on a winter morning. We ate three bowls each. The Manaïari considered this restrained. River went back for a fourth. She has been volunteering at the docks every morning this week. She has never shown interest in fishing before.

For the dragons perched on the high ledges, ice cream is delivered by catapult. Great frozen spheres launched into the air, caught mid-flight in open jaws. The aim is remarkably good. Remarkably, not perfectly. I watched one sphere sail wide and explode against a cliff face. A dragon who had nothing to do with the transaction licked the cliff for the rest of the afternoon.

Dragons also get brain freeze. The expression is unmistakable. Eyes wide, jaw frozen open, a look of profound betrayal directed at no one in particular. They do not learn from this. They never eat more slowly. I watched one dragon get brain freeze three times in a single sitting. Each time, it looked up at the sky as though the sky were somehow responsible.


## 63. Mirrors to Humanity [T4]

Dragons watch us. I don't mean with suspicion or hunger. I mean with interest. The same way a child watches an adult do something pointless and decides it must be worth doing.

Yesterday, a man fell asleep in his hammock after lunch. Nothing unusual. But a dragon had been watching from the ridge, and this morning, the same dragon had wedged itself between two palm trees in a cargo net stolen from the docks. It was swaying dangerously. It looked deeply uncomfortable and absolutely committed to the idea. By the end of the week, three more dragons had done the same. The Manaïari have started reinforcing their trees. River watched the whole thing unfold with an expression I can only describe as delighted recognition, as if she had always suspected the world worked this way and was pleased to be proven right. Manami was watching River. She said nothing, but she had the look of someone recognising a younger version of somebody she knows.


## 64. I Brought Them Here [T4]

I took Cendre and River to the hillside settlement today. The one with the fire dragon.

The village remembered me, which I did not expect. People nodded as we arrived. A woman brought us food before we asked. Cendre studied the fireproofed thatch with his hands behind his back, the way his father used to examine a bookshelf. The Hiccupper is still here. It came up to me. Crossed the clearing without hesitation, lowered its head, and nudged my hand with its snout. I have been gone eight years. It has not forgotten. I do not know how it knew me. By smell, perhaps. By something else, perhaps.

It has, in the eight years since I last saw it, grown considerably and learned some control. The scorch circle around its resting spot is smaller. ## 65. The Sea: The Surfers [T3-T4]

The Manaïari surf the way the British drink tea: constantly, without ceremony, as if life without it would be technically possible but not worth the bother. Children surf before they can run properly. Old women surf. The harbour master surfs on her lunch break, props her board against the dock wall, and goes back to directing the fleet with wet hair and no comment. A man carried a board under one arm and a baby under the other, handed the baby to someone on the beach, and paddled out without breaking stride. He caught the first wave of the day and rode it the full length of the cove without once shifting his weight. Effortless. I suspect he does this every morning.

The boards are carved from a pale, buoyant wood I cannot identify, shaped with blue stone tools and waxed with dragon tallow. Each one is slightly different. Some are enormous, whole-family boards, ridden by three or four people at once, laughing, shifting their weight together. The boards are stored upright in racks near the beach, dozens of them, sun-bleached and salt-stained. They look like a row of standing stones. Some have been repaired so many times there is almost nothing of the original left. A board, like a dragon's nest, becomes a record of its use.

Dragons surf too. They ride the waves belly-down, wings tucked, using their tails as rudders. The long, serpentine species are naturals. The stocky, heavy ones are not, but they try anyway, with a determination that borders on fury. One wiped out so spectacularly it created a secondary wave that the Manaïari children rode back to shore. Everyone cheered, including the dragon.

They surf together. Humans and dragons, side by side, riding the same wave, adjusting to each other's speed and line. A dragon drops in and a surfer adjusts. A surfer takes off late and a dragon slows to match. It is the same wordless conversation I see everywhere on this island, but faster, more physical, more joyful. The best of them ride in tandem, the human on a board, the dragon gliding just beneath the surface alongside, and they carve the same turn at the same moment.

There are waves that only dragons can ride. Enormous, deep-ocean swells that break against the outer cliffs. The Manaïari sit on the rocks and watch these the way one watches a sunset. The dragons ride them for what looks like hours, carving lines across faces of water the height of buildings.

Every morning, before the fishing starts, the first surfers are in the water. It is the quietest part of the day. I went once, at Manami's insistence. I cannot surf. I sat on the board in the shallows and watched the others. The water was warm. A dragon surfaced beside me, looked at me, and dived under again. I stayed for an hour. The Normandy coast was never like this. Nothing was ever like this. I did not catch a wave. I caught something else.

River, on her second visit, took to it immediately. She says she is learning. From what I can see, she has already learnt. Kai takes her out at dawn. They come back wet and grinning and speaking a language I do not think is entirely Manaïari.

## 67. Snow Dragon Snowball Fights [T4]

There is no snow on Aizomea, but that has not stopped the ice dragons from inventing snowball fights. They make their own, breathing frost onto the ground until a patch of mountainside is covered in a thick, crunchy white that has no business being in the middle of the Pacific. Then they pack it with their claws, test the weight, and stockpile ammunition behind ridges like small, scaled generals preparing for war. The fights are joyful, more pillow fight than battle, and they can last all afternoon. The Manaïari children join in, woefully outmatched but fearless. River was in the thick of it within minutes. The snowball that hit me was the size of a watermelon. The one that hit Cendre was larger. He took it with dignity. River, already covered in snow, cheered.

The migratory birds have learnt to avoid the area during play hours. The ones that haven't are pelted mid-flight, tumbling from the sky in a puff of feathers and indignation. The dragons catch them before they hit the ground. Not out of cruelty. Hunger. It is a game that doubles as lunch. Some would find it morally complicated. ## 69. Night [T3-T5]

When the sun sets, the world changes entirely. There is no artificial light here beyond fire and blue stone. The crafted stone glows faintly in the dark --- the pendants around every neck, the inlays in walls, the tools and cooking pots and thimbles. Enough to find your way. Not enough to read by. Colours disappear. Shapes simplify. The dragons become silhouettes. The first few nights, I was frightened. Then I realised the dark here is not empty. It is full.

Full of sound, mostly. Dragon breathing is a bass note under everything, slow and vast. Insects. The distant sea. The creak of wings overhead, the soft thud of something enormous landing carefully somewhere I cannot see. Some species are nocturnal, and they move through the dark by senses I do not have. On full-moon nights, the blue stone in the mountains and the paths glows brighter, as if responding to the light. I went walking once under a full moon and felt as though I were moving through a painting someone had forgotten to finish. All outline, no fill, and somehow more beautiful for it.

Sleep is different here too. No alarms. No schedules. People sleep when they are tired and wake when they are not. I fought this for months, trained as I am by decades of English routine. Then I gave in. I have not slept this well since I was a child. Back in England, the silence kept me awake. I had not realised how much I needed the sound of something enormous breathing through the wall.

Children here are not afraid of the dark. I find this remarkable. In England, children need nightlights. Here, a child will wander outside at midnight to look at the stars with the casual confidence of someone checking the weather. The dark is not the enemy. It is the other half of the day.

The house is quiet tonight. River is out. I am not worried. I am not anything, except perhaps a little envious of how simple it looks from the outside, and how complicated I know it must be on the inside.

## 72. Bedtime Stories [T5]

At night, when families gather inside and the storytelling begins, the dragons come closer. You can hear them settling into position outside, the soft scrape of scales against bark, a rustle overhead. They are trying to be discreet. They are not good at it.

One dragon arrives every evening at the same family's window. It settles into the garden, tucks its legs beneath itself, and tilts its head at exactly the angle of a child being read to. It has preferences. Adventure stories make its tail twitch. Sad stories make it huff and shift its weight. Stories involving food make it lick its lips (which is distracting for everyone).

The mother told me she skipped a night once. When she opened the shutters the next morning, the dragon was still there. It had not moved. It was looking at the dark window with the patient, devastated expression of someone who has been stood up. She has not skipped a night since. Next door, it is the father who tells the stories. He does all the voices.

There is a lullaby here that exists in two versions. The mothers sing one. The dragons hum the other. The melody is the same, adapted. A child falling asleep hears both, layered. I heard it from outside a window one evening and had to sit down.

## 74. Cloud-Weaving Wyrms [T5]

On slow mornings, when the mist is thick and nothing much is happening, certain dragons shape the fog. A rabbit. A fish. Something that might be a boat or might be a very fat bird. The sculptures hold for a few minutes, then the breeze takes them apart and the dragon starts again. They don't seem to mind the impermanence. If anything, it's the point. Manami and I have taken to watching them together from the ridge, two women with sore knees and no particular schedule.

Some dragons leave the island. We know this. They slip through the mist and roam the world for years, sometimes centuries. And if they can shape fog here, in this valley, what stops them doing it elsewhere? Every cloud-castle a child has pointed at from a garden. Every horse, every ship, every dragon in the sky. What if none of it was accidental? I have absolutely no evidence for this. It is probably nonsense.


## 75. Inner Life [T5]

I wonder sometimes if I am selfish. I chose this. I left my family for a continent of dragons. I know most people would not understand. I am not sure I understand. I tell myself I am doing this for science. I am not entirely lying.

The difficulty is that I love it here. I am happy. And every moment of happiness is undercut by the knowledge that it is built on absence. I am happy because I left. That equation has never balanced, and I have stopped trying to make it.

I have known Manami for over thirty years now. She was the first person who spoke to me here, and she may know me better than anyone alive. She has never once asked me to stay. She has never once asked me to explain why I leave. I think that is what friendship looks like when it has had enough time to stop performing.

There is a particular loneliness in knowing something extraordinary and being unable to tell anyone.

I walked to the ridge this morning, to my rock. It was warm, as always. I sat for a long time. My knees ache more than they used to. The climb is harder. I did not bring my brushes or my notebook. I just sat, and looked, and tried to memorise the colour of the light.

River has changed since we arrived. She moves differently here. She laughs more easily. She looks at this place as someone who has found where she was always meant to stand. I recognise this because I felt it too, thirty years ago, and I know what it costs. She does not know yet what it will cost. A mother sees this coming and cannot say a word.

We are packing to leave. There was a new shell on my desk this morning. A tiny one, white, perfectly spiralled. The row along the windowsill now stretches from one end to the other. I packed them all.

River is folding something carefully into her bag, something she did not arrive with. A piece of cloth, dark blue, that I have not seen before. She held it to her face for a moment before wrapping it. The sky outside is clear. We should have good weather for the crossing.

*Deux vies.* Two lives. I cannot have them at the same time. This is the only thing about Aizomea that hurts. (I am not counting the claws, the fire, or the tail the width of an oak tree that I must walk past every morning on my way to breakfast.)
