*14th July 1948*

My darlings, if you ever wonder where you get it from, read this.

I have, after considerable fieldwork, compiled an unofficial guide to the stages of dragon boredom. I offer it here as a public service.

Stage one is the tail. A bored dragon taps its tail against the ground in a slow, irregular rhythm, like a man drumming his fingers when the conversation has gone on too long. This is harmless. Stage two is rearrangement. The dragon begins moving nearby objects into patterns. Sticks. Pebbles. Someone's sandals. The patterns have no meaning. That is the point. Stage three is the stare. The dragon fixes its gaze on a specific building, or person, or goat, and does not look away. This can last hours. Stage four is the grin. If you see the grin, I am told, it is already too late.

I did not believe it until the laundry incident. A dragon, left unattended for a single afternoon, removed every piece of drying laundry in the village and arranged it into a single enormous nest on the roof of the meeting hall. Shirts, wraps, bedsheets, all sorted by colour. It sat in the middle looking tremendously pleased with itself. Getting the laundry back required negotiation. The dragon wanted three fish and a compliment. It got both.

A week later, another dragon spent a morning arranging fish bones, driftwood, and stolen cutlery into an elaborate structure on the village green. The Manaïari could not decide whether it was vandalism or art. They argued about it for days. The dragon, bored again by then, knocked the whole thing over and built a new one. It has been doing this for weeks. The village now has an involuntary rotating art gallery, and opinions remain divided.

The worst case I witnessed involved a dragon and a child, both bored, who found each other on a Tuesday morning. By noon they had redirected a stream into someone's kitchen. By mid-afternoon they had built a dam out of cooking pots. By evening they had taught a goat to climb onto a roof. The village held an emergency meeting. The dragon attended, looking innocent. The child did not attend. She was on the roof, with the goat.

The Manaïari solution to all of this is simple: give the dragon a job. Any job. One formerly destructive dragon is now the village bell. It bongs its own head against a rock at appointed hours. It takes this duty with tremendous seriousness, and has not rearranged anyone's laundry since. It has, however, developed a slight headache and an unmistakable sense of purpose.
