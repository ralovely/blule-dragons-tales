*11th May 1938*

Today I witnessed something few in the world have (I seem to be saying that often lately, but this one feels different): I saw a dragon hatch.

It was also one of the funniest scenes. In a stone courtyard hemmed in by house walls on three sides, children were playing a game I'd describe as a mix of rugby and football, with the added twist of trying to get as many bounces off the walls as possible. It took me a few minutes to realise the 'ball' was, in fact, an egg. A dragon egg.

This variety was bouncy. Like rubber. As the game picked up, the egg seemed to take on a life of its own, adding a hilarious randomness to proceedings. After one epic kick, it bounced straight back into the kicker, with what looked for all the world like intent. N.B. A dragon egg is not fragile. Someone ought to tell the museum men who cradle them like porcelain.

That's when an elder man, who had been watching with an amused yet attentive eye, interrupted the game. The egg lay still on the ground. The children, a few other villagers, and even a couple of dragons gathered around. The egg, quite round until now, began to change shape from the inside. A very sharp claw pierced through and tore a larger hole.

From it, an already chunky (though small) dragon walked out. Or perhaps stumbled its way out, fumbling, rolling, still drunk on the bouncing. Everyone burst into laughter. River, you would have loved this. Cendre, you would have tried to take it home. I must bring you here one day. Though I suppose that requires being able to come back. Or leaving, for that matter.

Talking more about dragons' births with the Manaïari, I realise it's as much about folklore and community as it is biology. After that chaotic moment, Manami and I sat for hours over what passes for tea here (a pale gold infusion of sun-dried cliff moss that tastes faintly of honey and smoke) as she shared other hatching stories. I filled six pages of notes. My zoology professors would have wept, or resigned.

There is one species, she said, that is 'grown' in a shared garden. The eggs are velvety pods, like large melons, and the whole village tends to them. Apparently, a great deal of singing is involved. One morning, a pod will 'bloom' into a light pink, silken flower, and a dragon lies sleeping at its heart. (The 'flower', Manami noted, is actually the tip of its tail).

For another, the egg simply contains... another egg. Which itself contains another. Each nested shell is smaller and more ornate than the last. While the first egg might look like a rock as large as our dining table, the dragon that emerges from the innermost one is no bigger than my thumb.

Then, there is the Stone-egg, so hard it must be sung open. When it's time, the mother starts with a low note, and the whole village joins in, tapping in rhythm until you can feel the ground vibrate. Only then does the egg unfold, petal by stone petal. Here in Aizomea, a dragon, it seems, is born into a chorus.
